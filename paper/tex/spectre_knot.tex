\begin{abstract}
The Spectre of Smith, Myers, Kaplan and Goodman-Strauss tiles the plane, but only non-periodically. We decorate its
straight-edged form $\Tile$ with fourteen ribbon pieces, the same on every tile, that continue straight across every
tile edge. We prove that in every tiling by copies of $\Tile$ of one handedness whose supertile hierarchy
covers the plane no strand of ribbon is closed, and that in the tiling grown from a nested chain of $\Delta$-supertiles
all the pieces join into a single strand, a two-way infinite path through every tile; we do not claim this for other
tilings. Every supertile connects the ribbon ends on its boundary in the same pattern; ribbons zigzag along a shared
boundary, so lengthening it by two edges changes nothing; and the side lengths at every level are reached from those at
level~$1$ in such steps. So finitely many computations at level~$1$ settle every level. The combinatorial core is
formalised in the Lean proof assistant. The proof, the programs, the formalisation and this text were produced by the
author working with an AI system. This paper has not been peer reviewed (\cref{sec:intro}).
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

A \emph{Celtic knot} is a pattern of interlaced ribbons~\cite{Bain,Cromwell1993}; the number of strands in finite
designs of this kind has been studied, for instance, by Fisher and Mellor~\cite{FisherMellor}. A \emph{knot tile} is a
polygon carrying ribbon segments that end on its edges, so that copies of the tile, placed edge to edge, link up into
longer ribbons. We ask for a knot tile whose ribbons, in a tiling of the whole plane, form one endless strand. In a
periodic tiling this cannot happen:
if the pieces formed one strand, a period $\tau$ would shift it along itself by some $k\neq0$ ($\tau^2$ moves every
tile, so $\tau$ cannot reverse it), leaving $|k|$ orbits of pieces under $\langle\tau\rangle$ but infinitely many of
tiles, as $\RR^2/\langle\tau\rangle$ has infinite area.
So we look at a tile that tiles only non-periodically.

In 2023 Smith, Myers, Kaplan and Goodman-Strauss described the \emph{hat}, an aperiodic monotile~\cite{SMKGS-hat}, and
then the \emph{Spectre}~\cite{SMKGS-spectre}: a shape that tiles the plane, admits only non-periodic tilings, and
admits only tilings in which all tiles have the same handedness, even when reflections are
allowed~\cite[Thm.~2.2]{SMKGS-spectre}. The Spectre is a curved-edge form of the polygon $\Tile$ (\cref{fig:tile},
\cref{sec:spectre}). For background on tilings and substitution see~\cite{GrunbaumShephard,BaakeGrimm}.

We give a knot tile on the Spectre that is \emph{mono-stranded}: in one explicit tiling all its pieces form a single
strand (\cref{thm:B}), and no tiling whose hierarchy covers the plane contains a closed strand (\cref{thm:A}). The
decoration (\cref{fig:tile}) puts one crossing point on each of the fourteen edges and joins the $28$ ribbon ends inside
the tile by fourteen pieces: twelve \emph{hooks}, each turning back round one vertex, and two pieces that cross each
other inside the tile. Across an edge, the ribbons continue straight through the crossing point.

Every tiling by $\Tile$ with tiles of one handedness (a \emph{homochiral} tiling) groups uniquely into a hierarchy of
larger and larger \emph{supertiles}~\cite[Thm.~2.2]{SMKGS-spectre}. We say that the hierarchy \emph{covers the plane}
if the supertiles containing some tile exhaust the plane. This does not depend on the tile: once a supertile containing
one tile contains another, the two tiles have the same supertiles from that level on. Otherwise the tiling splits into
two or more \emph{infinite supertiles}, unions of nested chains of supertiles, separated by a \emph{fault}
(\cref{sec:scope}).

\begin{mainthm}[The Daedalus Theorem]\label{thm:A}
In every homochiral tiling of the plane by $\Tile$ whose supertile hierarchy covers the plane, with every tile carrying
the decoration of \cref{fig:tile}, there is no closed strand: every strand is a two-way infinite path of ribbon pieces.
\end{mainthm}

Our second result is proved for one tiling, the \emph{nest tiling} $\N$, grown from a single tile $N_0$ by repeatedly
taking the $\Delta$-supertile $N_{b+1}$ that has $N_b$ as its child number~$1$ (\cref{sec:nest}). In the language of
substitution tilings, $\N$ is generated from one tile by iterating the substitution about it, a fixed point of the
substitution (in the combinatorial sense; compare~\cite{BaakeGrimm}); in the tile addresses
of~\cite[\S2.8]{TathamTransducers} (after~\cite{GoodmanStrauss1999}), $N_0$ has the address
$\Delta\xleftarrow{1}\Delta\xleftarrow{1}\Delta\xleftarrow{1}\cdots$.

\begin{mainthm}[The Ariadne Theorem]\label{thm:B}
In the nest tiling $\N$, with every tile carrying the decoration of \cref{fig:tile}, all the ribbon pieces of all the
tiles form a single strand. It is a two-way infinite path
that passes through every tile. More precisely, for every $b\ge 1$, all the ribbon arcs of the block $N_b$ lie on a
single arc of $N_{b+4}$, and of $N_{b+3}$ when $b$ is even.
\end{mainthm}

We make no claim about the number of strands in any other tiling: computations indicate that in at least one other
tiling whose hierarchy covers the plane the pieces form three strands; and tilings with faults are covered by neither
theorem (\cref{sec:scope}).

\subsection*{The method}
The proof has three ideas.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item \emph{Black boxes.} Seen from outside, a supertile is just a list of which ribbon end on its boundary is
joined to which. Every supertile of every kind and level $m\ge1$ has the same \emph{signature}: a hook at every boundary vertex
but two adjacent ones, $A$ and $B$, where the two ribbons cross over; and no closed strand inside (\cref{def:signature},
\cref{thm:signature}).
\item \emph{Zigzags.} When two such boxes are glued along a common boundary path, the ribbons zigzag along it, and the
outcome depends only on the parity of its length (\cref{lem:zigzag}). So lengthening a shared path by two edges changes
nothing (\cref{lem:lengthen}).
\item \emph{Fixed parities.} The supertiles are combinatorially hexagons and compose by the substitution
of~\cite[Fig.~5.1]{SMKGS-spectre}. Their side lengths are given by Fibonacci numbers (\cref{prop:fib}); they only grow
with the level, and their parities never change. So the side lengths at any level are reached from those at level~$1$
by lengthening, two edges at a time (\cref{lem:reach}), and a finite computation at level~$1$ gives the signature at
every level.
\end{enumerate}
The single strand (\cref{thm:merge}) follows the same pattern. Where the ribbons of a block go one level up depends only
on where they leave it, for most of its rim only on the side (\cref{lem:junctionmap}); this map is computed at
level~$1$ and carried to every level by the same lengthening. Followed three or four levels up, it puts every arc of a
block on one strand.
The finite computations (the level-$1$ data, the agreement with~\cite[Figs.~4.1, 5.1]{SMKGS-spectre}, and
\cref{comp:compose,comp:junction} on the $18$ compositions of level-$1$ supertiles) are exact; the last two are repeated
in the kernel of the Lean proof assistant (\cref{app:computations}). \Cref{fig:roadmap} shows how the steps fit
together, and which of them Lean checks.

\subsection*{Related work}
Curves drawn on tiles and joined across edges, each closed or two-way infinite as in \cref{lem:degree2}, have been
studied for random Truchet tilings~\cite{Pete2008,Hooper2011}; for the mirror curves of Celtic, Tchokwe and Tamil
designs~\cite{JablanKnotsArt}; for the Conway--Penrose arcs on Penrose rhombs, all closed apart from at most two (see~\cite{FlickerSimonParameswaran}
and the references there); and for Girih interlace on Penrose tilings~\cite{Cline2024}. Single curves through every
tile of finite patches have been built as Hamiltonian cycles on Ammann--Beenker tilings~\cite{SinghLloydFlicker} and
as space-filling curves from aperiodic substitution tilings~\cite{Pautze2021}. Closest to ours, an online
project~\cite{BohemianMiserSpectre} decorates selected edges of $\Tile$ and describes, in one nested tiling, a single
non-crossing curve through every tile, with a machine check of its combinatorial core. Here every edge carries a
ribbon end and the pieces cross;
in our drawing they cross only transversally and in pairs (\cref{app:computations}), so over and under can alternate by
the checkerboard rule~\cite{CromwellKnots}, and the single strand becomes an alternating diagram of a line, as in Celtic
knotwork.
We are not aware of earlier full knotwork decorations that form a single strand.

\section{Spectre tilings}\label{sec:spectre}

\subsection{The tile}
Let $\rho=e^{i\pi/6}$. The polygon $\Tile$ has fourteen unit edges; we label its vertices $v_0,\dots,v_{13}$
counter-clockwise and its edges $e_i=v_iv_{i+1}$ (indices mod $14$), with $v_0=0$ and
\[
v_{i+1}-v_i=\rho^{k_i},\qquad (k_0,\dots,k_{13})=(0,10,1,3,0,2,5,7,4,6,6,8,11,9).
\]
All vertices lie in $\ZZ[\rho]$. This is the equilateral member of the family $\mathrm{Tile}(a,b)$~\cite[\S\S1--2]{SMKGS-spectre},
with its long side split into the collinear edges $e_9,e_{10}$; it is the mirror image of the polygon drawn
in~\cite[Fig.~1.1]{SMKGS-spectre}, and has the handedness of the single Spectre and of the Mystic
in~\cite[Fig.~2.1]{SMKGS-spectre}. \emph{Spectres} are tiles, obtained for instance by replacing every edge of $\Tile$ by
a suitable curve, whose tilings correspond exactly, with the same isometries, to the \emph{homochiral} tilings by
$\Tile$, in which any two tiles are related by a rotation and a translation~\cite[\S2, Lemma~2.1]{SMKGS-spectre}; so
what~\cite{SMKGS-spectre} proves for tilings by Spectres holds for homochiral tilings by $\Tile$~\cite[p.~5]{SMKGS-spectre}. Such tilings
exist~\cite[Thm.~2.2]{SMKGS-spectre}, and in each, tiles meet full edge to full edge~\cite[proof of
Lemma~2.1]{SMKGS-spectre}. Reflecting the plane if necessary (with the decoration below, which maps strands to strands),
we assume that every tile is the image of $\Tile$ under a rotation and a translation. The tile has no non-trivial
symmetry, proper or improper (its cyclic sequence of edge directions has none), so an isometry between two tiles is
unique.

\subsection{Supertiles}
We use the hierarchy of~\cite{SMKGS-spectre}, with nine \emph{kinds}
$\K=\{\Gamma,\Delta,\Theta,\Lambda,\Xi,\Pi,\Sigma,\Phi,\Psi\}$. A \emph{level-$0$ supertile} of kind $K\neq\Gamma$ is a
single tile; one of kind $\Gamma$ is a \emph{Mystic}~\cite[Fig.~2.1]{SMKGS-spectre}, the pair of tiles
$\{\Tile,\ g(\Tile)\}$ with $g(z)=\rho z+v_8$, or a congruent copy, with \emph{first} tile $\Tile$. The first tile is
determined by the pair, since the Mystic has no non-trivial proper symmetry: one exchanging its tiles would be an
involution taking $\Tile$ to $g(\Tile)$, but the only proper motion doing so is $g$, a rotation by $30^\circ$. A
level-$(m+1)$ supertile is the union of level-$m$ supertiles, its \emph{children}: eight, or seven for kind $\Gamma$, of
the kinds listed in \cref{tab:children}. A level-$1$ supertile has the kind of its cluster
in~\cite[Fig.~4.1]{SMKGS-spectre}, which is determined by the positions of its six vertices~\cite[proof of
Thm.~4.1]{SMKGS-spectre}; a level-$(m+1)$ supertile, $m\ge1$, has kind $T$ if its children form the panel $T$
of~\cite[Fig.~5.1]{SMKGS-spectre}. Children are numbered as in \cref{fig:level1} at level $1$, and by their positions in
the last row of \cref{tab:children} above. A single tile gets the kind of its position in its level-$1$ supertile; only
the distinction between $\Gamma$ and the other kinds is used at level~$0$. Child~$1$ of every supertile is a $\Delta$
and the last child a $\Gamma$; at levels $m\ge1$ a $\Delta$ occurs only as child~$1$.

The level-$1$ supertiles are the Spectre clusters ($9$ tiles) and the Mystic cluster ($8$ tiles, kind $\Gamma$)
of~\cite[Fig.~2.1]{SMKGS-spectre}, with the markings of~\cite[Fig.~4.1]{SMKGS-spectre}, up to reflection
(\cite[Fig.~2.1]{SMKGS-spectre} draws them with tiles of the opposite handedness to $\Tile$, \cref{fig:level1} with
that of $\Tile$). They are combinatorially the marked hexagons of~\cite[Fig.~4.2]{SMKGS-spectre}, and
the level-$n$ supertiles of hexagons of~\cite[\S5]{SMKGS-spectre} are our level-$(n+1)$ supertiles.

The following theorem restates, in our language, results of~\cite{SMKGS-spectre}.

\begin{theorem}[Smith, Myers, Kaplan, Goodman-Strauss~\cite{SMKGS-spectre}]\label{thm:smkgs}
Let $\T$ be a homochiral tiling by $\Tile$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\alph*)}]
\item \textup{\cite[Thms.~2.2, 3.1, 4.1, \S5, Fig.~2.1]{SMKGS-spectre}} The tiles of $\T$ group uniquely into level-$1$
supertiles. Each contains one Mystic, in the position shown in~\cite[Fig.~2.1]{SMKGS-spectre}; this Mystic and the other
tiles, taken singly, are the level-$0$ supertiles inside it. For every $m\ge1$ the level-$m$ supertiles of $\T$ group
uniquely into level-$(m+1)$ supertiles. Each tile lies in a unique nested sequence $X_0\subset X_1\subset\cdots$ with
$X_m$ a level-$m$ supertile.
\item \textup{\cite[Thms.~3.1, 4.1, 5.1, \S5]{SMKGS-spectre}} For every $m\ge 1$, the tiling of the plane by the level-$m$
supertiles of $\T$ is combinatorially equivalent (homeomorphic as a topological complex~\cite[\S2.2]{SMKGS-spectre}) to a
tiling by regular hexagons, each level-$m$ supertile of kind $K$
corresponding to a hexagon marked as the hexagon $K$ of~\cite[Fig.~4.2]{SMKGS-spectre} for even $m$, and as its
mirror image for odd $m$, adjacent supertiles corresponding to hexagons sharing an edge with matching labels; and the
grouping into level-$(m+1)$ supertiles corresponds, up to reflection, to the grouping of hexagons shown
in~\cite[Fig.~5.1]{SMKGS-spectre}.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

In (a), \cite[Thm.~4.1]{SMKGS-spectre} gives the clusters and~\cite[Thm.~2.2]{SMKGS-spectre} the uniqueness at every
level, which we use only to speak of \emph{the} hierarchy of $\T$. In (b), the handedness is that of our level-$1$
supertiles, the mirror images of the clusters in~\cite[Figs.~2.1, 4.1]{SMKGS-spectre} (see the base case of ($\ast$) in
the proof of \cref{prop:pattern}); it alternates with $m$ by~\cite[Thm.~5.1]{SMKGS-spectre}.

In particular every level-$m$ supertile ($m\ge1$) is a closed topological disc whose boundary, its \emph{rim}, is a
simple closed polygon made of tile edges. Its \emph{vertices} are the six points of the rim where it meets two other
level-$m$ supertiles, and its \emph{sides} are the six arcs of the rim between consecutive vertices. (The tile
vertices on a rim are called \emph{rim vertices}; for a supertile of level $\ge1$, ``vertex'' alone always means one of
the six.) A vertex of one
supertile that lies on the rim of another is a vertex of that one too, since exactly three supertiles meet there. Each side is
shared, as a path of tile edges, with exactly one neighbouring supertile.

\subsection{Own orientation, side numbering and the crossover corner}\label{sec:own}
By~\cite[Thm.~5.1]{SMKGS-spectre}, the supertiles composed from a tiling by marked hexagons are combinatorially
equivalent to the \emph{mirror images} of those hexagons. So the cyclic order of the labelled sides reverses at each
level, and we orient supertiles alternately.

\begin{definition}[Own orientation]\label{def:own}
The \emph{own orientation} of a level-$m$ supertile is counter-clockwise if $m$ is even and clockwise if $m$ is odd.
We write $\omega_m=(-1)^m$.
\end{definition}

Thus a child and its parent are always oriented oppositely. Each supertile of level $m\ge1$ has a distinguished rim
vertex $A$, its \emph{crossover corner}, defined recursively: for a level-$1$ supertile, $A$ is the vertex $v_0$ of the
first tile of its Mystic; for a level-$(m+1)$ supertile, $A$ is the crossover corner of its $\Gamma$ child. (That this
point lies on the rim is computed at level $1$ and follows from \cref{prop:pattern}(d) at higher levels.) The second corner $B$,
adjacent to $A$, appears in \cref{thm:signature}; we call $A$ and $B$ the \emph{crossover corners}. The vertices of a
level-$m$ supertile ($m\ge1$) are numbered $0,\dots,5$ in own order, starting with the first vertex at or after $A$; side
$j$ runs from vertex $j$ to vertex $j+1$ (\cref{fig:level1}).

\begin{proposition}[The pattern holds at every level]\label{prop:pattern}
Let $\T$ be a homochiral tiling by $\Tile$, let $m\ge1$, and let $Y$ be a level-$(m+1)$ supertile of $\T$ of kind
$T$, with children $X_0,\dots,X_{n_T-1}$. Then:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\alph*)}]
\item $X_c$ has kind $K_c$ (\cref{tab:children});
\item side $j$ of $X_c$ is the same path of tile edges as side $j'$ of $X_{c'}$, traversed in the opposite direction,
whenever \cref{tab:sides} lists $c'.j'$, and it lies on the rim of $Y$ when the table lists $\rim$;
\item side $J$ of $Y$ is the concatenation of the children's sides listed in \cref{tab:psides}, and vertex $J$ of $Y$
is the vertex $j+1$ of the child $c$ whose side $c.j$ is listed first;
\item the crossover corner $A$ of $Y$ is vertex $0$ of its $\Gamma$ child, which is vertex $0$ of $Y$ if $T\neq\Sigma$, and
lies inside side $5$ of $Y$ if $T=\Sigma$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Justification]
By \cref{thm:smkgs}(b), the level-$m$ supertiles inside $Y$ are combinatorially the hexagons of the panel $T$
of~\cite[Fig.~5.1]{SMKGS-spectre}, with hexagon edges as sides and hexagon vertices as vertices. We use the dictionary
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item[($\ast$)] side $j$ of a level-$m$ supertile of kind $K$ carries the label of the edge of hexagon $K$ in direction
W, SW, SE, E, NE, NW for $j=0,\dots,5$ (\cref{tab:letters}); its crossover corner $A$ is vertex~$0$ if $K\neq\Sigma$; if
$K=\Sigma$, $A$ is not a vertex, and lies in the relative interior of side~$5$,
\end{itemize}
and show ($\ast$) by induction on $m$. For $m=1$ it is a computation on the level-$1$ supertiles, whose vertices and
corner $A$ are marked in \cref{fig:level1}. Drawn with the edges in odd directions lengthened to $\sqrt3$, which turns the tiles into hats and
turtles~\cite[\S3]{SMKGS-spectre}, and in the handedness of~\cite[Fig.~4.1]{SMKGS-spectre}, they coincide with the nine
marked clusters there: the same outlines, the vertices at the marked dots, the labels of \cref{tab:letters} where that
figure has them, and $A$ the dot between the sides labelled W and NW (for $\Sigma$, a point of the NW side), on the light
tile of the Mystic (checked by a script; \cref{app:computations}). Suppose ($\ast$) holds for the children of $Y$. The
panel does not label its inner hexagons, but the matching rules fix the rotation of each, and there is exactly one
consistent choice (\cref{app:computations}). With it, \cref{tab:sides,tab:psides} transcribe the panel, including its six
marked vertices; this gives (a)--(c). The crossover corner of $Y$ is that of its $\Gamma$ child, the child's vertex~$0$
by ($\ast$), which \cref{tab:psides} places at the start of the parent's side~$0$ if $T\neq\Sigma$ and inside side~$5$ if
$T=\Sigma$; this is (d). Finally, the labels that the panel assigns to the parent's sides, numbered from this point in
the parent's own order, are those of \cref{tab:letters} for every $T$. So ($\ast$) holds for $Y$, and in particular $A$
lies on the rim.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Rigidity]\label{lem:rigid}
Any two level-$m$ supertiles of the same kind, in possibly different homochiral tilings, are related by a rotation
and translation that maps descendants to descendants with their numbers (children, their children, and so on, down
to the tiles), and it is the only such motion. For $m\ge1$ it maps the six vertices, the sides and the corner $A$ of one onto those of the other.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Induction on $m$. For $m=0$ this is clear, since a tile and a Mystic have no non-trivial proper symmetry. For $m=1$,
the level-$1$ supertiles are congruent copies of the clusters of \cref{fig:level1}~\cite[Fig.~2.1, \S4]{SMKGS-spectre},
with their vertices at the points marked in~\cite[Fig.~4.1]{SMKGS-spectre}, their children numbered as in
\cref{fig:level1}, and $A$ fixed by the Mystic. Let $m\ge1$ and let $Y,Y'$ be level-$(m+1)$ supertiles of kind $T$. By
induction, child $c$ of $Y$ is mapped onto child $c$ of $Y'$, with its numbered vertices, by a unique proper motion
$g_c$. If side $j$ of child $c$ is glued to side $j'$ of child $c'$, then by \cref{prop:pattern}(b), in $Y$ and in $Y'$,
vertices $j$ and $j+1$ of $c$ are vertices $j'+1$ and $j'$ of $c'$; so $g_c$ and $g_{c'}$ agree on two distinct points
and are equal. The children's adjacency graph is connected, so all the $g_c$ are one motion $g$; it maps descendants to
descendants with their numbers and, by \cref{prop:pattern}(c),(d), the vertices, sides and corner $A$ of $Y$ onto those
of $Y'$. A proper motion that maps descendants to descendants maps the first tile of the Mystic at the bottom of the
chain of last ($\Gamma$) children to its counterpart, so it equals $g$, since a tile has no symmetry.
\end{proof}

Write $E_m(K)$ for the number of rim edges (equally, rim vertices) of a level-$m$ supertile of kind $K$, and
$\ell_m(K,j)$ for the number of edges of its side $j$. By \cref{lem:rigid} these numbers are well defined.

\section{The decoration and its strands}\label{sec:decoration}

\subsection{Ports and pieces}
Let $t$ be a tile and $e$ one of its edges, with endpoints $x$ and $y$. The tile has two \emph{ports} on $e$, the
port \emph{near $x$} and the port \emph{near $y$}; formally, they are the pairs $(e,x)$ and $(e,y)$. In the drawing
(\cref{fig:tileports}), $e$ carries one crossing point, and the two ribbon ends of $t$ that meet it, one running off
towards $x$ and one towards $y$, are these two ports. For a vertex $v_i$ of the tile, write
\[
\Rp(v_i)=\text{the port on } e_i \text{ near } v_i,\qquad \Lp(v_i)=\text{the port on } e_{i-1} \text{ near } v_i ,
\]
the ports just after and just before the corner $v_i$ in counter-clockwise order. Each of the $28$ ports is
$\Rp(v_i)$ or $\Lp(v_i)$ for exactly one $i$.

\begin{definition}[The decoration]\label{def:decoration}
The \emph{decoration} joins the $28$ ports of $\Tile$ in pairs by fourteen \emph{pieces}:
\[
\underbrace{\Lp(v_i)\leftrightarrow\Rp(v_i)\quad(2\le i\le 13)}_{\text{twelve hooks}},\qquad
\underbrace{\Rp(v_0)\leftrightarrow\Rp(v_1),\qquad \Lp(v_0)\leftrightarrow\Lp(v_1)}_{\text{the crossover}}.
\]
Every tile of a homochiral tiling carries the image of this decoration.
\end{definition}

Each hook turns back round one vertex; the two crossover pieces cross once inside the tile. Only the pairing of
\cref{def:decoration} matters for the theorems; any drawing that realises it may be used.

\subsection{The straight-through rule}
Let $\T$ be a homochiral tiling, and let $e$ be an edge shared by tiles $t$ and $t'$, with endpoints $x$ and $y$.

\begin{definition}[Straight-through rule]\label{def:rule}
The port of $t$ on $e$ near $x$ is \emph{joined} to the port of $t'$ on $e$ near $y$, and the port of $t$ near $y$ to
the port of $t'$ near $x$.
\end{definition}

This is what a ribbon does when it continues straight through the crossing point. In our drawing the crossing point of
$e_i$ is at distance $0.5+0.15(-1)^i$ from $v_i$, so the two tiles' crossing points on $e$ coincide: $e_i$ is always
glued to some $e_j$ with $i+j$ odd, by the vertex colouring of~\cite[proof of Lemma~2.1]{SMKGS-spectre}. The proofs use only
\cref{def:decoration,def:rule}.

\subsection{Strands}\label{sec:strands}
\begin{definition}[Strand graph]\label{def:strandgraph}
Let $\T$ be a homochiral tiling. The \emph{strand graph} $\G(\T)$ is the multigraph with one vertex for each piece of
each tile and one edge for each pair of ports joined by the straight-through rule, between the two pieces having
these ports as ends. A \emph{strand} is a connected component of $\G(\T)$; a strand is \emph{closed} if it is finite.
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:degree2}
Every vertex of $\G(\T)$ has degree $2$. Hence every strand is either a finite cycle (a closed strand) or a two-way
infinite path.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
A piece has two ends, each a port of its tile on some edge $e$. In a tiling of the plane, $e$ is shared by exactly one
other tile, so each end is joined to exactly one port, an end of a piece of that neighbour. So every vertex has degree exactly $2$;
joins always link pieces of different tiles, so $\G(\T)$ has no loops. A connected, locally finite
multigraph with all degrees $2$ is a finite cycle (possibly of length $2$) or a two-way infinite path.
\end{proof}

Equivalently, in a tiling of the plane the ends of the pieces and the straight-through rule pair the ports of all the
tiles by two involutions without fixed points, and the ports of a strand form an orbit of the group they generate. This
is how the formalisation describes strands.

\section{Boxes and the signature}\label{sec:boxes}

\subsection{Patches and boxes}
A \emph{patch} is a finite set $P$ of tiles of a homochiral tiling whose union is a closed disc with a simple polygonal
boundary, the \emph{rim} $\rim P$. Every supertile of level $\ge1$ is a patch (\cref{thm:smkgs}), and so is a tile.
Every rim edge belongs to exactly one tile of $P$. Let $E=E(P)$ be the number of rim edges, and number the rim vertices
$u_0,\dots,u_{E-1}$ in a chosen orientation of the rim, indices mod $E$; for a supertile we always use its own
orientation. For a rim vertex $u_k$ define the \emph{rim ports}
\[
\Rp(u_k)=\text{the port on } u_ku_{k+1} \text{ near } u_k,\qquad \Lp(u_k)=\text{the port on } u_{k-1}u_k \text{ near } u_k,
\]
where the port belongs to the tile of $P$ containing that edge. There are $2E$ rim ports. For the tile itself, with
its counter-clockwise orientation, these are the ports $\Rp(v_i),\Lp(v_i)$ of \cref{sec:decoration}. Reversing the
orientation exchanges $\Rp$ and $\Lp$ at every vertex.

\begin{lemma}[The box of a patch]\label{lem:box}
Start at a rim port of $P$ and follow the pieces of the tiles of $P$, crossing every interior edge of $P$ by the
straight-through rule. The path ends at a different rim port. This defines an involution $\pi_P$ on the rim ports
without fixed points. The pieces of $P$ that are not reached from any rim port form finitely many cycles, the
\emph{closed strands of $P$}.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The pieces of $P$ and the joins across interior edges form a finite graph in which every piece has degree $2$, except
that a piece end at a rim port has no partner. Its components are therefore paths, whose two ends are rim ports, and
cycles. Paths have two distinct ends.
\end{proof}

We call the paths of \cref{lem:box} the \emph{arcs} of $P$; there are exactly $E$ of them. The rim of $P$ with the
pairing $\pi_P$ is the \emph{box} of $P$. Everything we need about the inside of $P$ is in its box, together with
whether $P$ has a closed strand:

\begin{lemma}[Gluing]\label{lem:gluing}
Let a patch $P$ be the union of patches $P_1,\dots,P_n$ with pairwise disjoint interiors. Then the box of $P$ is
determined by the boxes of the $P_i$ together with the identification of their shared rim edges. More precisely,
$\pi_P$ is computed by alternately applying the $\pi_{P_i}$ and the straight-through rule across shared edges, until a
rim port of $P$ is reached. The closed strands of $P$ are those of the $P_i$ together with the cycles of this
alternation that contain no rim port of $P$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Every arc or closed strand of $P$ (\cref{lem:box}) is a concatenation of arcs and closed strands of the $P_i$, joined
across edges shared by two of the $P_i$.
\end{proof}

We use \cref{lem:gluing} in two ways: to define the box of a composition, glued from boxes alone (\cref{sec:comp}), and
to compute the box of a supertile from those of its children.

\subsection{The signature}
\begin{definition}[Signature]\label{def:signature}
Let $A$ and $B$ be adjacent rim vertices of a patch $P$. We say that $P$ has the \emph{signature at $(A,B)$} if
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item $\pi_P(\Lp(u))=\Rp(u)$ for every rim vertex $u\notin\{A,B\}$ (a \emph{hook} at $u$);
\item $\pi_P(\Rp(A))=\Rp(B)$ and $\pi_P(\Lp(A))=\Lp(B)$ (the \emph{crossover});
\item $P$ has no closed strand: the ribbon followed from any port of $P$ reaches the rim.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

The definition is symmetric in $A$ and $B$ and does not depend on the orientation of the rim, since reversing it
exchanges $\Lp$ and $\Rp$. A patch with the signature has $E-2$ hook arcs, one at each rim vertex other than
$A$ and $B$, and two crossover arcs. \Cref{fig:signature} is a picture.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:tilesig}
A tile has the signature at $(v_0,v_1)$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
A tile has no interior edge, so $\pi$ is the pairing of \cref{def:decoration}, and every piece has both ends on the rim.
\end{proof}

\section{The Zigzag Lemma}\label{sec:reduce}

In this section a patch $P$ is the union of patches (\emph{children}) $X_0,\dots,X_{n-1}$ with disjoint interiors, each
$X_c$ having the signature at some $(A_c,B_c)$. All are oriented in the same rotational sense, so two children traverse
a common rim path in opposite directions; $\Rp_c(u),\Lp_c(u)$ are the rim ports of $X_c$ at $u$ in this orientation.
(For a supertile and its children in their own orientation, $P$ is oriented opposite to its own orientation, which
exchanges $\Lp$ and $\Rp$ at the parent's rim vertices but does not affect the signature.)

\begin{lemma}[Zigzag Lemma]\label{lem:zigzag}
Let $u_0u_1\cdots u_n$ ($n\ge1$) be a path of $n$ edges lying on the rims of both $X_c$ and $X_{c'}$, traversed by $X_c$
from $u_0$ to $u_n$. Suppose that none of the interior vertices $u_1,\dots,u_{n-1}$ is a crossover corner of $X_c$ or of
$X_{c'}$. Then, alternately applying the straight-through rule across the edges $u_ku_{k+1}$ and the hooks of $X_c$ and
$X_{c'}$ at $u_1,\dots,u_{n-1}$, the ports at the two ends of the path are joined as follows:
\begin{align*}
n \text{ even:}\quad & \Rp_c(u_0)\leftrightarrow \Lp_c(u_n), && \Rp_{c'}(u_n)\leftrightarrow \Lp_{c'}(u_0);\\
n \text{ odd:}\quad & \Rp_c(u_0)\leftrightarrow \Rp_{c'}(u_n), && \Lp_c(u_n)\leftrightarrow \Lp_{c'}(u_0).
\end{align*}
These two connections use every port on the edges of the path, so no closed strand lies along the path.
\end{lemma}

path at every edge and turn back at every corner, alternately in $X_c$ (above) and $X_{c'}$ (below). For odd $n$ each

\begin{proof}
Start at $\Rp_c(u_0)$, the port of $X_c$ on $u_0u_1$ near $u_0$. The straight-through rule takes it to the port of
$X_{c'}$ on $u_0u_1$ near $u_1$. In the orientation of $X_{c'}$, which runs from $u_1$ to $u_0$ along this edge, that
port is $\Rp_{c'}(u_1)$. If $n=1$ we have reached $\Rp_{c'}(u_n)$. Otherwise $u_1$ is a hook of $X_{c'}$, which leads
to $\Lp_{c'}(u_1)$, the port of $X_{c'}$ on $u_1u_2$ near $u_1$. Crossing $u_1u_2$ leads to the port of $X_c$ on
$u_1u_2$ near $u_2$, which is $\Lp_c(u_2)$. If $n=2$ we stop. Otherwise the hook of $X_c$ at $u_2$ leads to $\Rp_c(u_2)$,
and the pattern repeats with all indices shifted by two. So the path visits the hooks of $X_{c'}$ at odd $k$ and the
hooks of $X_c$ at even $k$. It ends at $\Lp_c(u_n)$ if $n$ is even and at $\Rp_{c'}(u_n)$ if $n$ is odd. The same
argument from the other end, with $X_c$ and $X_{c'}$ exchanged (and, for odd $n$, $\Lp$ and $\Rp$), starting at
$\Rp_{c'}(u_n)$ for even $n$ and at $\Lp_c(u_n)$ for odd $n$, gives the second connection; it visits the remaining
hooks. Each connection crosses each of the $n$ edges once, using two of its four ports, and the two connections use
different ports, so together they use all of them.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Letters}

\begin{proposition}[Lengths by letter]\label{prop:letters}
For every $m\ge1$ the length $\ell_m(K,j)$ depends only on the letter of side $j$ of $K$. Writing $x_m$ for the common
length of the sides with letter $x$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rec}
\begin{aligned}
\alpha_{m+1}&=\beta_m+\epsilon_m+\eta_m, &\qquad \beta_{m+1}&=\alpha_m+\epsilon_m,\\
\gamma_{m+1}&=\beta_m+2\epsilon_m+\eta_m+\theta_m, & \delta_{m+1}&=\alpha_m+\beta_m+\epsilon_m+2\theta_m,\\
\epsilon_{m+1}&=3\epsilon_m+\eta_m, & \zeta_{m+1}&=\alpha_m+\epsilon_m+\theta_m,\\
\eta_{m+1}&=4\epsilon_m+\eta_m, & \theta_{m+1}&=4\epsilon_m+\eta_m+\theta_m,
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
with $(\alpha_1,\beta_1,\gamma_1,\delta_1,\epsilon_1,\zeta_1,\eta_1,\theta_1)=(7,5,10,10,8,6,10,11)$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For $m=1$ this is a direct count on the level-$1$ supertiles (\cref{fig:level1}; \cref{app:computations}). By
\cref{prop:pattern}(c), $\ell_{m+1}(T,J)$ is the sum of the lengths of the children's sides listed in
\cref{tab:psides}. Inspection of \cref{tab:children,tab:psides,tab:letters} shows that the multiset of letters of these children's
sides depends only on the letter of side $J$ of $T$, and is the one in~\eqref{eq:rec}. For example, side $0$ of
$\Delta$ (letter $\gamma$) is made of $7.5,6.4,6.3,6.2,5.4$, with letters $\beta,\eta,\epsilon,\epsilon,\theta$. The
claim follows by induction on $m$.
\end{proof}

The recurrence~\eqref{eq:rec} has a closed form. Let $\Fib_n$ be the Fibonacci numbers, $\Fib_1=\Fib_2=1$.

\begin{proposition}[Fibonacci side lengths]\label{prop:fib}
For every $m\ge1$,
\begin{gather*}
\alpha_m=\Fib_{3m+2}+2,\qquad \beta_m=\Fib_{3m+1}+2,\qquad \gamma_m=\delta_m=\eta_m=2\Fib_{3m+2},\\
\epsilon_m=\Fib_{3m+3},\qquad \zeta_m=2\Fib_{3m+1},\qquad \theta_m=\Fib_{3m+4}-2 .
\end{gather*}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For $m=1$, $(\Fib_4,\Fib_5,\Fib_6,\Fib_7)=(3,5,8,13)$ gives the initial values. For the induction step put
$a=\Fib_{3m+1}$ and $b=\Fib_{3m+2}$, so that $\Fib_{3m+3}=a+b$, $\Fib_{3m+4}=a+2b$, $\Fib_{3m+5}=2a+3b$,
$\Fib_{3m+6}=3a+5b$ and $\Fib_{3m+7}=5a+8b$. Substituting into~\eqref{eq:rec}:
\begin{gather*}
\alpha_{m+1}=(a+2)+(a+b)+2b=\Fib_{3m+5}+2,\qquad \beta_{m+1}=(b+2)+(a+b)=\Fib_{3m+4}+2,\\
\gamma_{m+1}=(a+2)+2(a+b)+2b+(a+2b-2)=4a+6b=2\Fib_{3m+5},\\
\delta_{m+1}=(b+2)+(a+2)+(a+b)+2(a+2b-2)=4a+6b,\\ \eta_{m+1}=4(a+b)+2b=4a+6b,\qquad
\epsilon_{m+1}=3(a+b)+2b=\Fib_{3m+6},\\ \zeta_{m+1}=(b+2)+(a+b)+(a+2b-2)=2\Fib_{3m+4},\\
\theta_{m+1}=4(a+b)+2b+(a+2b-2)=5a+8b-2=\Fib_{3m+7}-2.\qedhere
\end{gather*}
\end{proof}

Since $\Fib_n$ is even exactly when $3\mid n$:

\begin{corollary}[Parities are constant]\label{cor:parity}
For every $m\ge1$ the lengths $\alpha_m$, $\beta_m$, $\theta_m$ are odd and $\gamma_m$, $\delta_m$, $\epsilon_m$,
$\zeta_m$, $\eta_m$ are even.
\end{corollary}

\subsection{The crossover corner}
\begin{lemma}[Position of $A$]\label{lem:Apos}
Let $m\ge1$. For $K\neq\Sigma$ the crossover corner $A$ of a level-$m$ $K$-supertile is its vertex $0$. For
$K=\Sigma$ it lies on side $5$ (letter $\delta$), at distance
\[
d_m=2\Fib_{3m+1}
\]
from vertex $5$ and at distance $\delta_m-d_m=2\Fib_{3m}$ from vertex $0$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
For $K\neq\Sigma$ this is \cref{prop:pattern}(d) for $m\ge2$, and is read off \cref{fig:level1} for $m=1$. For
$m=1$, $d_1=6$ is read off the level-$1$ $\Sigma$-supertile. By \cref{prop:pattern}(d) and
\cref{tab:psides}, the crossover corner of a level-$(m+1)$ $\Sigma$-supertile follows the children's sides $0.2$,
$0.1$, $7.0$ on its side~$5$. Child $0$ is a $\Xi$ and child $7$ a $\Gamma$, so these sides have letters
$\theta,\epsilon,\alpha$ and
$d_{m+1}=\theta_m+\epsilon_m+\alpha_m=(a+2b-2)+(a+b)+(b+2)=2\Fib_{3m+4}$, in the notation of the previous proof.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Lower bounds]\label{lem:bounds}
For every $m\ge1$: every side has length at least $5$; $d_m\ge6$; and $\delta_m-d_m\ge4$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
By \cref{prop:fib} all letter lengths increase with $m$, and at $m=1$ the smallest is $\beta_1=5$. Also $d_m\ge d_1=6$ and
$\delta_m-d_m=2\Fib_{3m}\ge 2\Fib_3=4$.
\end{proof}

\section{The signature at every level}\label{sec:induction}

We prove the Signature Theorem by induction on the level, reducing every step to a finite computation at level~$1$.

\subsection{Compositions as a function of side lengths}\label{sec:comp}
Fix a parent kind $T$, with children $c=0,\dots,n_T-1$ of kinds $K_c$ (\cref{tab:children}), one of which, $c_\Sigma$, is
a $\Sigma$. Let $L$ assign a positive integer $L(c,j)$ to each child $c$ and side $j\in\{0,\dots,5\}$, equal on sides
glued by \cref{tab:sides}; let $d$ be an integer with $2\le d\le L(c_\Sigma,5)-2$, and $\omega\in\{\pm1\}$. The
\emph{composition} $C_T(L,d,\omega)$ is the following abstract patch of boxes. Child $c$ has a rim of
$E_c=\sum_jL(c,j)$ vertices, numbered $0,\dots,E_c-1$ in its own order, with side $j$ running from vertex
$V_c(j)=\sum_{i<j}L(c,i)$ to vertex $V_c(j+1)$; its crossover corner $A_c$ is vertex $0$, or for $c=c_\Sigma$ the
vertex at distance $d$ from vertex $5$ along side $5$; $B_c=A_c+\omega$; and it is a box with the signature at
$(A_c,B_c)$. Sides are glued in pairs as in \cref{tab:sides}, reversing direction: the $o$-th edge of side $j$ of $c$ is
the $o$-th edge from the end of side $j'$ of $c'$. The sides marked $\rim$ form the rim of the composition, in the order
of \cref{tab:psides} read backwards (the table lists them in the parent's own order); the ports of a rim vertex are those
of the children on its two rim edges. Following ribbons alternately through a child, by its signature, and straight
across a glued edge, as in \cref{lem:gluing}, defines the box of the composition. The Zigzag Lemma and its proof apply
unchanged to compositions, since they use only the signatures of the children and the straight-through rule. We say that
$C_T(L,d,\omega)$ \emph{has the signature} if it has no closed strand, and every rim vertex is a hook except the
crossover corners $A_\Gamma,B_\Gamma$ of its $\Gamma$ child, which are adjacent rim vertices ($B_\Gamma$ following
$A_\Gamma$ in the children's order if $\omega=+1$, and preceding it if $\omega=-1$) and carry the crossover.

Write $\ell_m$ also for the function $(c,j)\mapsto\ell_m(K_c,j)$. If the children of a level-$(m+1)$ $T$-supertile have
the signature at $(A_c,A_c+\omega_m)$ in their own orientation, then the supertile is glued from them as
$C_T(\ell_m,d_m,\omega_m)$ is. Indeed: (i)~by \cref{prop:pattern}(a),(b) and \cref{prop:letters} the children have the
kinds and side lengths of the composition, and side $j$ of $X_c$ is side $j'$ of $X_{c'}$ traversed oppositely; (ii)~the
rims of two children meet only in the closed sides they share: at a point inside a side both rim edges are shared with
the partner, so the two children fill a neighbourhood, and a vertex lies on exactly three level-$m$ supertiles, any two
of which share a side ending there (\cref{thm:smkgs}(b)); (iii)~so each side of a child is wholly shared with one
sibling or wholly on the rim, as in \cref{tab:sides}; (iv)~$A_c$ is placed by \cref{lem:Apos}, and $B_c=A_c+\omega_m$ by
hypothesis; (v)~the rim of the parent is given by \cref{tab:psides} (\cref{prop:pattern}(c)). By \cref{lem:gluing}, the
strands of the supertile are then those of the composition, and its box is the box of $C_T(\ell_m,d_m,\omega_m)$.
Step~(ii) is the one geometric step; with \cref{thm:smkgs,prop:pattern} it is part of the assumption~(M1) of
\cref{app:ai}. The compositions themselves are built from the tables alone, which agree with each other:
\cref{tab:psides} lists each side that \cref{tab:sides} puts on the rim exactly once, and turning about the end of each
entry, across the sides that \cref{tab:sides} glues, leads to the start of the next (a finite check, made in the
formalisation).

\subsection{Lengthening}\label{sec:lengthen}
Let $(L,d)$ be as above. A \emph{move} lengthens one side of one child by two edges, together with the side glued to it
if there is one. An edge $e$ of that side is replaced by a path of three edges through two new vertices; $e$ must not
be the first or the last edge of its side (in each child that contains it), nor one of the two edges at $A_\Sigma$
(the crossover corner of the $\Sigma$ child). The result is $(L',d')$, where $L'$ is $L$ with the side (and its
partner) two edges longer, and $d'=d+2$ if $e$ lies on side $5$ of the $\Sigma$ child before $A_\Sigma$, and $d'=d$
otherwise.

\begin{lemma}[Lengthening]\label{lem:lengthen}
If $C_T(L,d,\omega)$ has the signature, then so does $C_T(L',d',\omega)$, for every move $(L,d)\to(L',d')$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Write $C=C_T(L,d,\omega)$ and $C'=C_T(L',d',\omega)$, and let $e=u_0u_1$. Then $C'$ is $C$ with $e$ cut into three: in
each child that contains it, $e$ is replaced by the path $u_0v_1v_2u_1$, and every vertex of $C$ has an image vertex in
$C'$ (positions after $e$ move on by two). The conditions on $e$ make the corners of $C'$ the images of those of $C$:
for $A_\Sigma$ by the choice of $d'$, and $B_c=A_c\pm1$ stays next to $A_c$ because the edge between them (the first edge
of side $0$, the last of side $5$, or an edge at $A_\Sigma$) is never cut. In particular $v_1$ and $v_2$ are not crossover
corners. Let $\varphi$ map each port of $C$ to the port of $C'$ of the same hand at the image vertex. Then $\varphi$
commutes with the pairings inside the children, since the corners correspond, and with the straight-through rule across
every glued edge other than $e$. If $e$ is shared by children $c$ and $c'$, crossing it joins $\Rp_c(u_0)$ to
$\Rp_{c'}(u_1)$ and $\Lp_c(u_1)$ to $\Lp_{c'}(u_0)$ (\cref{lem:zigzag} with $n=1$); in $C'$, by \cref{lem:zigzag} with
$n=3$, the ribbons run along the new path from $\varphi(\Rp_c(u_0))$ to $\varphi(\Rp_{c'}(u_1))$ and from
$\varphi(\Lp_c(u_1))$ to $\varphi(\Lp_{c'}(u_0))$. If $e$ lies on the rim, $v_1$ and $v_2$ are hooks of the child, and so
new rim vertices of $C'$ that are hooks of $C'$.

Hence, for every port $x$ of $C$, the ribbon of $C'$ from $\varphi(x)$ passes through the images of the ports met from
$x$ in $C$, and reaches the rim at the image of the rim port reached in $C$. So $C'$ has a hook at the image of every rim
vertex of $C$ except $A_\Gamma$ and $B_\Gamma$, and a hook at each new rim vertex; $A_\Gamma$ and $B_\Gamma$ are adjacent
as before, are still rim vertices, since the rim edges of the $\Gamma$ child at them stay on the rim, and carry the
crossover. Finally, $C'$ has no closed strand: every port of $C'$ at an old vertex is some $\varphi(x)$, whose
ribbon reaches the rim; and a strand through a port at $v_1$ or $v_2$ is a hook of the rim, or follows one of the two
connections of \cref{lem:zigzag} along $u_0v_1v_2u_1$, which use every port on these edges and end at ports at $u_0$
and $u_1$, so it too reaches the rim.
\end{proof}

Call $(L,d)$ \emph{admissible} if sides glued by \cref{tab:sides} have the same
length; $L(c,j)\ge\ell_1(c,j)$ and $L(c,j)\equiv\ell_1(c,j)\pmod2$ for all $c$ and $j$; $d\ge d_1$ and $d\equiv
d_1\pmod2$; and $L(c_\Sigma,5)-d\ge\ell_1(c_\Sigma,5)-d_1=4$. Then $d\ge6$ and $L(c_\Sigma,5)-d\ge4$, so the composition
$C_T(L,d,\omega)$ is defined.

\begin{lemma}[Reachability]\label{lem:reach}
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\alph*)}]
\item For every $m\ge1$, the pair $(\ell_m,d_m)$ is admissible.
\item Every admissible $(L,d)\neq(\ell_1,d_1)$ arises by a move from an admissible pair $(L_0,d_0)$ with
$\sum_{c,j}L_0(c,j)<\sum_{c,j}L(c,j)$. The move can be chosen so that its edge $e$ lies at least two edges from both
ends of its side, in each child that contains it, unless that side is side $5$ of the $\Sigma$ child or is glued to
it.
\end{enumerate}
Hence a property of pairs that holds at $(\ell_1,d_1)$ and is kept by the moves of \textup{(b)} holds at every
admissible pair, in particular at $(\ell_m,d_m)$ for every $m\ge1$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
(a) Glued sides have the same letter (\cref{sec:lengths}), so the same length. By \cref{prop:fib} the letter lengths do
not decrease with $m$, and by \cref{cor:parity} their parities do not change. By \cref{lem:Apos}, $d_m=2\Fib_{3m+1}\ge
6=d_1$ is even, and $\ell_m(c_\Sigma,5)-d_m=\delta_m-d_m=2\Fib_{3m}\ge4$.

(b) If $L=\ell_1$, then $L(c_\Sigma,5)=\delta_1=10$ and $d_1\le d\le 10-4$, so $d=d_1$. Hence $L\neq\ell_1$, and by the
parity condition some side $j$ of some child $c$ has $L(c,j)\ge\ell_1(c,j)+2$; we undo a move on that side. If the side
is neither side $5$ of $\Sigma$ nor glued to it, take $d_0=d$ and the move whose edge is the third edge of the side (the
third from the end in the partner): before the move the side has length at least $\ell_1(c,j)\ge5$ (\cref{lem:bounds}),
so this edge lies at least two edges from both ends, in either child. Otherwise, measure positions along side $5$ of the
$\Sigma$ child from its vertex $5$. If $d\ge d_1+2$, take $d_0=d-2$ and the move whose edge is the second edge of the
side, before $A_\Sigma$; it does not touch $A_\Sigma$, since $d_0\ge6$. If $d=d_1$, take $d_0=d$ and the second edge
after $A_\Sigma$: before the move the side has $L(c_\Sigma,5)-2-d\ge\ell_1(c_\Sigma,5)-d_1=4$ edges after $A_\Sigma$,
so this edge is at least two edges from the end of the side. In each case $(L_0,d_0)$ is admissible, and
$\sum L_0=\sum L-2$ or $\sum L-4$. The last statement follows by induction on $\sum_{c,j}L(c,j)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{computation}[The compositions at the level-$1$ lengths]\label{comp:compose}
For each of the nine kinds $T$ and both signs $\omega$, the composition $C_T(\ell_1,d_1,\omega)$ has the signature.
\end{computation}

This was computed by a program that builds the $18$ compositions from \cref{tab:children,tab:sides,tab:psides} and the
lengths alone and follows the ribbon from every port, and again in the kernel of Lean (\cref{app:computations}).

\begin{proposition}[Compositions at every level]\label{prop:compose}
For every $m\ge1$, every kind $T$ and both signs $\omega$, the composition $C_T(\ell_m,d_m,\omega)$ has the signature.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Fix $T$ and $\omega$. The property that $C_T(L,d,\omega)$ has the signature holds at $(\ell_1,d_1)$ by
\cref{comp:compose}, and is kept by every move by \cref{lem:lengthen}. By \cref{lem:reach} it holds at $(\ell_m,d_m)$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The induction}\label{sec:induct}
\begin{computation}[Base case]\label{comp:base}
Every level-$1$ supertile has the signature at $(A,B)$, where $A$ is as in \cref{lem:Apos} and $B=A+\omega_1$ in own
order. Moreover $\ell_1$ and $d_1$ are as stated in \cref{prop:letters,lem:Apos}.
\end{computation}

This was computed from the tiles in exact arithmetic, with \cref{lem:gluing}, checking the statement as worded, and again
by a second program and in the kernel of Lean (\cref{app:computations}).

\begin{theorem}[Signature Theorem]\label{thm:signature}
In every homochiral tiling by $\Tile$, for every $m\ge1$, every level-$m$ supertile of every kind has the signature at
$(A,B)$, where $A$ is its crossover corner (\cref{sec:own}), placed as in \cref{lem:Apos}, and $B=A+\omega_m$ in its own
order. In particular no such supertile contains a closed strand.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
For $m=1$ this is \cref{comp:base}. Assume the theorem for level $m\ge1$ and let $Y$
be a level-$(m+1)$ supertile of kind $T$. Its children have the signature at $(A_c,A_c+\omega_m)$, so
by \cref{sec:comp} (with \cref{lem:gluing}) its box is that of $C_T(\ell_m,d_m,\omega_m)$, which has the signature by
\cref{prop:compose}.
Hence $Y$ has no closed strand, and hooks at all its rim vertices except the adjacent crossover corners
$A_\Gamma,B_\Gamma$ of its $\Gamma$ child, which carry the crossover. By definition $A_\Gamma$ is the crossover corner of
$Y$, placed as in \cref{lem:Apos}. Finally $B_\Gamma=A_\Gamma+\omega_m$ in the child's own order, which is opposite to
that of $Y$; so $B=A-\omega_m=A+\omega_{m+1}$ in the own order of $Y$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{No closed strands}\label{sec:noclosed}
\begin{proof}[Proof of \cref{thm:A}]
Let $\T$ be a homochiral tiling whose hierarchy covers the plane: for some tile $t$, the supertiles
$X_0(t)\subset X_1(t)\subset\cdots$ containing $t$ have union $\RR^2$. Write $X_m=X_m(t)$. First, about points of the
plane: every tile $t'$ lies in some $X_m$, and every edge of $t'$ is shared by two tiles of some $X_m$. Indeed, a point
$x$ of the interior of $t'$ lies in $X_m$ for some $m$, hence in some tile $s$ of $X_m$. Every neighbourhood of $x$ meets
the interior of $s$, so the interiors of $s$ and $t'$ meet, and $s=t'$. An edge of $t'$ is shared with exactly one other
tile (\cref{lem:degree2}), and the two tiles lie in some $X_m$.

The rest is combinatorial. Let $X_1\subset X_2\subset\cdots$ be supertiles, each a child of the next, such that every
edge of every tile of their union is shared by two tiles of some $X_m$; we show that every strand of the union is a
two-way infinite path. A closed strand has finitely many pieces, in finitely many tiles, and is joined across finitely
many edges, so these all lie in a single $X_m$, each edge shared by two of its tiles. It is then a closed strand of
$X_m$, contradicting \cref{thm:signature}. By the argument of \cref{lem:degree2}, every strand is a two-way infinite
path. This second step is \cref{thm:A} as the formalisation states it (\cref{app:ai}).
\end{proof}

\section{The nest tiling}\label{sec:nest}

Child~$1$ of every supertile is a $\Delta$ (\cref{tab:children}).

\begin{construction}[The nest]\label{con:nest}
Let $\T$ be a homochiral tiling~\cite[Thm.~2.2]{SMKGS-spectre}. For each $h\ge0$, let $Y^{(h)}$ be a level-$h$ $\Delta$-supertile
of $\T$; one exists, for instance child $1$ of any level-$(h+1)$ supertile. Let
$Y^{(h)}=Y^{(h)}_h\supset Y^{(h)}_{h-1}\supset\cdots\supset Y^{(h)}_0$ be its chain of descendants, each child $1$ of
the next. Let $g_h$ be the proper motion taking the tile $Y^{(h)}_0$ onto $\Tile$ (with $v_0$ at the origin), and put
$N^{(h)}_b=g_h(Y^{(h)}_b)$ for $b\le h$.
\end{construction}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:nestwell}
$N^{(h)}_b$ does not depend on $h\ge b$ (or on the choices made). Writing $N_b$ for it, $N_0=\{\Tile\}$, and $N_b$ is
child $1$ of $N_{b+1}$ for every $b$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let $h,h'\ge b$, with $Y^{(h)}$, $Y^{(h')}$, $g_h$, $g_{h'}$ as in \cref{con:nest}. By \cref{lem:rigid} there is a proper motion $f$ with $f(Y^{(h)}_b)=Y^{(h')}_b$ that maps descendants to
descendants with their numbers; hence $f(Y^{(h)}_{b'})=Y^{(h')}_{b'}$ for all $b'\le b$, in particular for the tiles
$Y_0$. So $g_{h'}f$ and $g_h$ both take the tile $Y^{(h)}_0$ onto $\Tile$, and since a tile has no symmetry they
are equal. Thus $N^{(h')}_b=g_{h'}f(Y^{(h)}_b)=N^{(h)}_b$.
\end{proof}

The \emph{nest tiling} is $\N=\bigcup_{b\ge0}N_b$, a set of tiles.

\begin{lemma}[Grandparent Lemma]\label{lem:gap}
Let $b\ge1$. \textup{(a)} The block $N_b$ does not meet the rim of $N_{b+2}$. \textup{(b)} Every edge of a tile of
$N_b$ is shared by two tiles of $N_{b+2}$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
(a) $N_b$ is child $1$ of $N_{b+1}$ (\cref{prop:nestwell}). By \cref{tab:sides}, the sides $0,1,3,4,5$ of child $1$ face
the siblings $7,0,2,3,4$, and side $2$ lies on the parent's rim. A point of $N_b$ that is not on its closed side $2$ is
therefore an interior point of $N_{b+1}$. This is clear for interior points of $N_b$ and for interior points of the
other sides, where $N_b$ and one sibling together fill a neighbourhood. At the vertices $0,1,4,5$ of $N_b$, exactly three
level-$b$ supertiles meet; two of them are the distinct siblings across the adjacent sides, and together they fill a
neighbourhood. It remains to treat the closed side $2$ of $N_b$. By \cref{tab:psides} (the $\Delta$ row: positions $14$
to $18$), side $4$ of the parent is the concatenation of $2.2,2.1,1.2,0.4,0.3$. Since $1.2$ is neither first nor last,
the closed side $2$ of $N_b$ lies in the relative interior of side $4$ of $N_{b+1}$. In $N_{b+2}$, side $4$ of its child
$N_{b+1}$ faces the sibling $3$, so, as above one level up, the points of its relative interior are interior points of
$N_{b+2}$.

(b) A rim edge of $N_b$ lies on a side $j\ne2$ of $N_b$, which faces a sibling inside $N_{b+1}$, or on side $2$, which
lies on side $4$ of $N_{b+1}$ (position $16$ of \cref{tab:psides}); and side $4$ of child $1$ faces sibling $3$ inside
$N_{b+2}$ (\cref{tab:sides}). Any other edge of a tile of $N_b$ is shared by two tiles of $N_b$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:nesttiling}
$\N$ is a homochiral tiling of the plane, and every tile of $\N$ lies in some $N_b$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Any two tiles of $\N$ lie in a common block, so their interiors are disjoint. We show that the tiles cover the plane.
Fix $h$ and work in the homochiral tiling $\T_h=g_h(\T)$ of \cref{con:nest}, which contains $N_0,\dots,N_h$
(\cref{prop:nestwell}). Call two tiles of $\T_h$
\emph{neighbours} if they share a point, and let $\operatorname{dist}$ be the resulting graph distance. We claim that every
tile at distance at most $k$ from $N_1$ lies in $N_{1+2k}$, provided $1+2k\le h$. For $k=0$ there is nothing to prove.
If $t$ has distance $k+1$, it shares a point $p$ with a tile of $N_{1+2k}$. By \cref{lem:gap}, $p$ is an interior point of
$N_{3+2k}$, so the interior of $t$ meets the interior of a tile of $N_{3+2k}$; hence $t$ is that tile.
Now let $x\in\RR^2$ and let $D$ be the disc of radius $r=|x|+1$ about the origin. The tiles of $\T_h$ meeting $D$ have
diameter $3+\sqrt3<5$ and area $3+3\sqrt3>8$, so there are at most $k_0=\lfloor\pi(r+5)^2/8\rfloor$ of them. They cover
the connected set $D$, so their neighbour graph is connected. One of them is $N_0\subset N_1$, since the origin is a
vertex of $N_0$. So every tile of $\T_h$ meeting $D$, in particular one containing $x$, lies in $N_{1+2k_0}$ as soon as
$h\ge 1+2k_0$. Since $N_{1+2k_0}$ does not depend on $h$, $x$ is covered by $\N$.
\end{proof}

The blocks $N_b$ are supertiles in the sense of \cref{sec:spectre}: each is congruent, with all its structure, to a
level-$b$ $\Delta$-supertile of $\T$. So \cref{thm:signature} applies to them. In particular $\N$ has no closed strand: a
closed strand passes through finitely many tiles, which lie in a common block $N_b$, $b\ge1$, by \cref{prop:nesttiling};
by \cref{lem:gap}(b) every edge of these tiles is shared by two tiles of $N_{b+2}$, so it would be a closed strand of
$N_{b+2}$.

\begin{lemma}[The junction map]\label{lem:junctionmap}
Let $m\ge1$. In $N_{m+1}$, every hook of $N_m$ at a rim vertex strictly inside side $2$ of $N_m$ is a hook of
$N_{m+1}$, and every other arc of $N_m$ lies on the hook $H_q$ of $N_{m+1}$, where $q$ is given by
\cref{tab:junction}.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The first part needs no computation. Side $2$ of $N_m$ is the side at position $16$ of \cref{tab:psides}, so it lies on
the rim of $N_{m+1}$, and a rim vertex $u$ of $N_m$ strictly inside it is a rim vertex of $N_{m+1}$, with the same two
rim ports. By \cref{lem:junctions}, $u$ lies inside side $4$ of $N_{m+1}$, so it is not a crossover corner, and by
\cref{thm:signature} it is a hook of $N_{m+1}$; this hook is the hook of $N_m$ at $u$.

For the rest, say that $(L,d)$ has the \emph{junction property} if the statement of \cref{comp:junction} holds for
$C_\Delta(L,d,\omega)$ for both $\omega$. By \cref{thm:signature} and \cref{sec:comp}, $N_{m+1}$ is glued from its
children as $C_\Delta(\ell_m,d_m,\omega_m)$ is, with $N_m$ as child $1$; a ribbon of the composition from a port $p$ of
child $1$ runs along the arc of $N_m$ ending at $p$, and on along the arc of $N_{m+1}$ that contains it; and a hook arc
has both its ends at one rim vertex. So the rest of the lemma says that $(\ell_m,d_m)$ has the junction property. By
\cref{comp:junction}, $(\ell_1,d_1)$ has it, so by \cref{lem:reach} it suffices to show that the moves of
\cref{lem:reach}(b) keep it. Since child $1$ is a $\Delta$, and the side glued to side $5$ of the $\Sigma$ child
(child $4$) belongs to child $7$ (\cref{tab:sides}), such a move whose edge lies on the rim of child $1$ keeps it at
least two edges from both ends of its side.

Let $C$, $C'$ and $\varphi$ be as in the proof of \cref{lem:lengthen}. Junctions and the vertices $V_j$ of child $1$ are
vertices of children, so those of $C'$ are the images of those of $C$, and $\varphi$ keeps the order of positions on the
rim of child $1$. For a port $x$ of child $1$ at an old vertex, the ribbon from $\varphi(x)$ reaches the rim at the image
of the rim port reached from $x$, and $\varphi(x)$ lies in the same row of \cref{tab:junction} as $x$. It remains to
consider the new vertices $v_1,v_2$ when the edge $e=u_0u_1$ lies on the rim of child $1$. Then $v_1,v_2,u_0,u_1$ lie
strictly inside the same side of child $1$, at least two edges from its ends, so in the same row of
\cref{tab:junction}, and none of them is a crossover corner. If that side is side $2$, the property says nothing about
$v_1$ and $v_2$. Otherwise $e$ is shared with a sibling $c'$. Write $\Rp_1,\Lp_1$ and $\Rp_{c'},\Lp_{c'}$ for the rim
ports of the two children, and let child $1$ run from $u_0$ to $u_1$. From $\Lp_1(v_2)$, the hook of child $1$ at $v_2$
and one crossing lead to $\Rp_{c'}(u_1)$, as in $C$ the hook at $u_0$ and the crossing of $e$ lead there from
$\Lp_1(u_0)$. From $\Rp_1(v_2)$, the hook of child $1$ at $v_2$, the hook of $c'$ at $v_1$ and two crossings lead to
$\Rp_1(u_0)$. In both cases the ribbon then runs on as in $C$, under $\varphi$, so it reaches the rim where the ribbon of
$C$ from the port of the same hand at $u_0$ does. Likewise at $v_1$, with $u_1$ in place of $u_0$. Those ports lie in
the same row, so the property holds at $v_1$ and $v_2$ too.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Where the junctions lie]\label{lem:junctions}
Let $m\ge1$. The junctions $9$, $12$, $16$, $17$ and $21$ of $N_{m+1}$ lie on its sides $2$, $3$, $4$, $4$ and $5$
respectively, at least two edges from both ends of the side. Every rim vertex strictly inside side $2$ of $N_m$ lies on
side $4$ of $N_{m+1}$, at least two edges from both ends.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
By \cref{tab:psides}, side $2$ of a $\Delta$ is made of the children's sides at positions $7$ to $10$, side $3$ of
those at $11$ to $13$, side $4$ of those at $14$ to $18$, and side $5$ of those at $19$ to $21$. Junction $q$ lies
between the sides at positions $q-1$ and $q$, which lie in the same side of $N_{m+1}$ for $q=9,12,16,17,21$; and side $2$
of $N_m$ is the side at position $16$. Every side of a supertile of level $\ge1$ has at least five edges
(\cref{lem:bounds}).
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of \cref{thm:merge}]
A rim vertex of $N_m$ at least two edges from both ends of its side $J$ is not a crossover corner, and by
\cref{lem:junctionmap} its hook lies on the hook of $N_{m+1}$ that \cref{tab:junction} names for side $J$: for even
$m$, junction $17$, $17$, $16$, $12$, $9$ for $J=0,1,3,4,5$; for odd $m$, junction $21$, $17$, $16$, $16$, $16$; and
for $J=2$, the vertex is itself a hook of $N_{m+1}$, on its side $4$. By \cref{lem:junctions}, each of these hooks of
$N_{m+1}$ lies at least two edges inside a side of $N_{m+1}$, so the same rule applies to it one level up.

Let $b\ge1$ be even, so that $\omega_b=+1$. By \cref{lem:junctionmap} every arc of $N_b$ lies on $H_{17}$, $H_{16}$,
$H_{12}$ or $H_9$ of $N_{b+1}$, or on a hook of $N_{b+1}$ strictly inside side $2$ of $N_b$; by \cref{lem:junctions}
these are hooks inside sides $4$, $4$, $3$, $2$ and $4$ of $N_{b+1}$. Since $b+1$ is odd, the hooks inside sides $3$ and
$4$ lie on $H_{16}$ of $N_{b+2}$, and those inside side $2$ are hooks of $N_{b+2}$ inside its side $4$. All are inside
side $4$ of $N_{b+2}$, and since $b+2$ is even they lie on $H_{12}$ of $N_{b+3}$. So $\mathcal P_3(b)$ has a single
block.

Let $b\ge1$ be odd. Every arc of $N_b$ lies on $H_{21}$, $H_{17}$ or $H_{16}$ of $N_{b+1}$, or on a hook of $N_{b+1}$
strictly inside side $2$ of $N_b$: inside sides $5$, $4$, $4$, $4$ of $N_{b+1}$. Since $b+1$ is even, those inside side $4$
lie on $H_{12}$ and those inside side $5$ on $H_9$ of $N_{b+2}$, inside its sides $3$ and $2$. Since $b+2$ is odd, the
first lie on $H_{16}$ of $N_{b+3}$, and the second are hooks of $N_{b+3}$ inside its side $4$. All are inside side $4$ of
$N_{b+3}$, and since $b+3$ is even they lie on $H_{12}$ of $N_{b+4}$. So $\mathcal P_4(b)$ has a single block. Once
$\mathcal P_k(b)$ has a single block, so does $\mathcal P_{k'}(b)$ for every $k'\ge k$.

The same steps give the exactness. For even $b$ the arcs of $N_b$ lie on two arcs of $N_{b+2}$: $H_{16}$, and the hook
at junction $9$ of $N_{b+1}$, which lies inside side $2$ of $N_{b+1}$ and so is not junction $16$ of $N_{b+2}$ (an end of
that side). For odd $b$ they lie on $H_{12}$ and $H_9$ of $N_{b+2}$, and on $H_{16}$ of $N_{b+3}$ and the hook at
junction $9$ of $N_{b+2}$, which for the same reason is not junction $16$ of $N_{b+3}$. Both arcs are reached in each
case (for instance from vertices well inside sides $4$ and $5$ of $N_b$, or sides $4$ and $0$), and distinct hooks are
distinct arcs.
\end{proof}

\subsection{One strand}\label{sec:onestrand}
\begin{proof}[Proof of \cref{thm:B}]
By \cref{lem:degree2}, every component of the strand graph $\G(\N)$ is a cycle or a two-way infinite path, and there
are no cycles, as shown after \cref{prop:nesttiling}. It remains to show that $\G(\N)$ is connected.
Let $p,q$ be pieces of tiles $t,t'$ of $\N$. By \cref{prop:nesttiling} both tiles lie in some $N_b$ with $b\ge1$. Since
$N_b$ has no closed strand, $p$ and $q$ lie on arcs of $N_b$. By \cref{thm:merge} these arcs lie on one arc of $N_{b+4}$,
so $p$ and $q$ are on the same strand. The strand passes through every tile, since every tile has pieces.
\end{proof}

\section{Scope and open questions}\label{sec:scope}

\Cref{thm:B} is proved for the nest tiling, whose chain of supertiles is $\Delta\subset\Delta\subset\cdots$ with child
number $1$ at every step. Every tiling whose hierarchy covers the plane has some chain $(K_m,c_m)_{m\ge1}$, where $X_m$
is a level-$m$ supertile of kind $K_m$ and child $c_m$ of $X_{m+1}$. The induction of \cref{sec:induction} does not see
the chain, but the merging of \cref{sec:strand} does.
Computations, not used here, indicate that the outcome depends on it. Along the chain $K_m=\Theta$, $c_m=2$ (odd $m$),
$K_m=\Gamma$, $c_m=7$ (even $m$), built as in \cref{con:nest} and covering the plane by the argument of
\cref{lem:gap,prop:nesttiling}, the arcs of $X_1$ lie on exactly three arcs of $X_m$ for $3\le m\le8$ (exact
arithmetic), and still on three at $m=20$ (a symbolic count); we have not proved that this persists. Along $90{,}000$ chains chosen at random, the arcs of $X_b$ ($b\le3$) lay on a
single arc of $X_m$ for some $m\le20$ in all but five (folder \code{experiments/}).
Which tilings whose hierarchy covers the plane carry a single strand, and which numbers of strands occur, is open.

In a tiling with a \emph{fault}, the supertiles containing a tile fill only part of the plane, an \emph{infinite
supertile}~\cite[\S5]{TathamTransducers}, and two or more of these meet along the fault; Tatham~\cite[\S7.3]{TathamTransducers}
gives an example. A closed strand inside one infinite supertile is ruled out by \cref{thm:signature}: its finitely many
tiles lie in one supertile, and every edge it crosses is shared by two of them, so it is a closed strand of that
supertile. One crossing the fault is not reached by the argument. Whether such a strand can occur is open.
\Cref{thm:B} is not affected, since the nest tiling has no fault (\cref{prop:nesttiling}).

The theorems use only the pairing of ports (\cref{def:decoration}) and the straight-through rule, so they hold for any
drawing that realises these, including on the curved Spectre. For another decoration with the signature, the same
finite computations can be run; their outcome may differ.

\section{The computations}\label{app:computations}

All programs and their logs are in the ancillary files of this paper, folder \code{code/}: \code{./run\_all.sh}
re-runs everything the proof needs, and \code{code/README.md} maps every statement of this paper to the script that
checks it. The Lean formalisation is in the folder \code{lean/} (\cref{app:ai}).

\emph{What the proof computes.} The geometric computations on actual supertiles (the level-$1$ data and
\cref{comp:base}; the patterns of the compositions $m\to m+1$ for $m\le3$) are done in exact
arithmetic by \code{exact/exact.py}, with every vertex in $\ZZ[\rho]$ and every motion $z\mapsto\rho^kz+t$; floating
point is used only to read off the sign of an area exceeding $8$ in absolute value. The computations on compositions
(\cref{comp:compose,comp:junction}) use only \cref{tab:children,tab:sides,tab:psides} and the integers $\ell_1$, $d_1$:
\code{hierarchy/compositions.py} builds each composition, follows the ribbon from every port and checks the statement
as worded. The Lean kernel repeats \cref{comp:compose,comp:junction,comp:base}, and
runs two negative controls as tests, which the programs run too: with each child's corner $B_c$ two vertices from
$A_c$, all $18$ checks of \cref{comp:compose} fail; with the junctions $12$ and $16$ exchanged in \cref{tab:junction},
\cref{comp:junction} fails for both signs. For \cref{prop:pattern}, a script fits our level-$1$ supertiles to a render
of~\cite[Fig.~4.1]{SMKGS-spectre} (the six dots of each cluster by a proper similarity with residual below $1\%$ of an
edge, every other matching or kind at least $40$ times worse; the outline, the Mystic's shading; the labels, read by
hand), and another encodes the nine panels of~\cite[Fig.~5.1]{SMKGS-spectre}, finds the one consistent rotation of each
inner hexagon and checks the encoding against \cref{tab:sides,tab:psides}.

\emph{Evidence, not proof.} Besides \cref{rem:patterncheck}, separate programs, mostly in JavaScript, sharing no code
with \code{exact.py} and working in floating point (we do not bound their rounding error), agree with the proof:
the actual supertiles up to level~$9$ have the signature;
following every ribbon through the actual blocks up to level~$9$ gives the numbers of \cref{tab:merge}, and
\cref{tab:junction} holds on $N_1,\dots,N_4$ (exact arithmetic);
and second routes, through \emph{skeletons} of the compositions (only the parities of the shared runs)
and a symbolic computation with certified linear inequalities, give \cref{thm:signature,thm:merge} again.
The drawn ribbons realise the pairing of \cref{def:decoration} on all supertiles up to level~$3$; each piece stays
inside its tile, the two crossover pieces cross once, transversally, and the pieces meet each edge at $\pm45^\circ$, so
the curves cross only in pairs.

\section{Use of AI, formal verification and tools}\label{app:ai}

\subsection*{How the work was done}
The author came up with the original idea of looking for a mono-stranded aperiodic knot, and then used an AI system
(Claude, by Anthropic) to search for it; from the $34$ candidates found, the author chose design $0.1{:}01$ and drew its
ribbons by hand. From then on the author and the AI system worked together: the AI system did the detailed work (the
programs, the proof, the Lean formalisation and this paper), and the author directed, guided and corrected it. The
author has read parts of this paper and spot-checked figures and counts; no professional mathematician has reviewed
it. The AI system reviewed its own argument in several passes. A program or check called \emph{independent} or
\emph{separate} here is a separate implementation by the same AI system. A fuller account:
\url{https://cortado-j.github.io/SPECTRE_KNOT/made.html}.

\subsection*{Formal verification}
The combinatorial part of the proof is formalised in the Lean~4 proof assistant~\cite{Lean4}, using its core library
only (folder \code{lean/}). In a combinatorial form, in which a patch, or a whole tiling, is described by which edge of
which tile is glued to which, Lean's kernel has checked \cref{thm:signature} for every level $m\ge1$; \cref{thm:A} for
the union of any chain of supertiles, each a child of the next, in which every edge is eventually glued (the second step
of its proof; the nest is such a chain); \cref{thm:B} for the nest tiling built as the union of the blocks $N_b$, with
\cref{thm:merge}, its exactness included; and \cref{lem:gap}, part (a) at the vertices of tiles. The formal statements
rest on \cref{tab:children,tab:sides,tab:psides} and on the nine level-$1$ supertiles as glued patches of tiles, computed
in exact arithmetic. The link with tilings of the plane is a stated assumption, in three parts: (M1)~the supertiles of an
actual tiling are glued as the tables say (\cref{thm:smkgs}, \cref{prop:pattern,lem:rigid}, and step~(ii) of
\cref{sec:comp}); (M2)~a tiling whose hierarchy covers the plane is the union of such a chain (the first step of the
proof of \cref{thm:A}); (M3)~\cref{prop:nestwell,prop:nesttiling}, with the step of \cref{lem:gap}(a) from the
vertices of tiles to the points of the plane. The formal proofs follow
\cref{sec:decoration,sec:boxes,sec:reduce,sec:lengths,sec:induction,sec:nest,sec:strand} step by step
(\code{lean/README.md} maps each result to its formal counterpart), with differences of bookkeeping only: strands are
orbits of ports (\cref{sec:strands}); the signature is stated by following ribbons (\cref{def:signature}); and inside the
proofs of \cref{lem:lengthen,prop:compose} a composition's rim vertices are found by turning about a vertex of a child,
which cutting an edge leaves unchanged, with the positions of \cref{tab:psides} and the adjacency of $A$ and $B$
recovered at the end. The formalisation also proves that a lengthened composition is the one with an edge cut into
three, and facts the paper takes from the geometry: every supertile is a disc at its rim, and its gluing is symmetric and
glues no tile to itself. The proofs use only Lean's standard axioms. The formalisation was written by the AI system as well; its statement file
(\code{lean/SpectreKnot/Statement.lean}) contains every definition that the formal theorems depend on, with comments in
plain English. No human has checked that these formal statements say what the theorems of this paper say.
