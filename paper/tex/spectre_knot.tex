\begin{abstract}
The Spectre of Smith, Myers, Kaplan and Goodman-Strauss tiles the plane, but only non-periodically.
We decorate its straight-edged form $\Tile$ with fourteen ribbon pieces, the same on every tile, so that ribbons
continue straight across every tile edge. We prove that the pieces never close up: in every tiling by copies of $\Tile$ of one handedness whose supertile
hierarchy covers the plane, the ribbons contain no closed strand. In the tiling grown from a nested chain of
$\Delta$-supertiles, all the pieces of all the tiles join into a single strand, a two-way infinite path that passes
through every tile. We do not claim this for other tilings, and computations indicate that it fails in some.
The proof shows that every supertile, at every level, connects the ribbon ends on its
boundary in the same simple pattern: a hook at every boundary vertex but two, and one crossover. Where supertiles are
glued, the ribbons zigzag along the shared boundary, so lengthening it by two edges changes nothing; and the side
lengths at every level are reached from those at level~$1$ in steps of two, because every side length is a Fibonacci
number, twice one, or one plus or minus two, with the same parity at every level. This reduces the infinite problem to
finitely many cases at level~$1$, which we check by computer. The single strand is reduced in the same way to a map,
computed at level~$1$, that says where the ribbons of a supertile go one level up. The combinatorial core of the
proof, with these computations, is also formalised in the Lean proof assistant. The proof, the programs, the
formalisation and this text were produced by the author working with an AI system (see \emph{Use of AI and
verification status} in \cref{sec:intro}).
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

A \emph{Celtic knot} is a pattern of interlaced ribbons~\cite{Bain,Cromwell1993}. How many separate strands a finite
design of this kind contains has been studied, for instance, by Fisher and Mellor~\cite{FisherMellor}. A \emph{knot
tile} is a polygon carrying ribbon segments that end on its edges, so that copies of the tile, placed edge to edge,
link up into longer ribbons. We ask for a knot tile whose ribbons, in a tiling of the whole plane, form one endless
strand. In a periodic tiling this cannot happen: if the decorated tiling is invariant under a non-zero translation, its
ribbons never form a single strand (\cref{rem:periodic}). So a tile that only tiles
non-periodically is the natural place to look.

In 2023 Smith, Myers, Kaplan and Goodman-Strauss described the \emph{hat}, an aperiodic monotile~\cite{SMKGS-hat}, and
then the \emph{Spectre}~\cite{SMKGS-spectre}: a shape that tiles the plane, admits only non-periodic tilings, and
admits only tilings in which all tiles have the same handedness, even when reflections are
allowed~\cite[Thm.~2.2]{SMKGS-spectre}. For background on tilings and substitution
see~\cite{GrunbaumShephard,BaakeGrimm}.
The Spectre is a curved-edge form of the polygon $\Tile$ (\cref{fig:tile}): tilings by Spectres correspond exactly to
the tilings by $\Tile$ in which all tiles have the same handedness~\cite[\S2]{SMKGS-spectre}.

This paper describes a knot tile on the Spectre and proves that it is \emph{mono-stranded} in the following sense:
in one explicit tiling, all its pieces form a single strand (\cref{thm:B}), and no tiling whose hierarchy covers the
plane contains a closed strand (\cref{thm:A}). We do not claim one strand for every tiling; computations indicate that some tilings have three (\cref{rem:three}). The decoration
(\cref{fig:tile}) puts one crossing point on each of the fourteen edges and joins the $28$ ribbon ends inside the tile
by fourteen pieces: twelve \emph{hooks}, each turning back round one vertex, and two pieces that cross each other
inside the tile. Across an edge, the ribbons simply continue straight through the crossing point.

Our first result concerns all tilings with a well-behaved hierarchy. Every tiling by $\Tile$ with tiles of one
handedness (a \emph{homochiral} tiling; \cref{sec:spectre}) groups uniquely into a hierarchy of larger and larger
\emph{supertiles}~\cite[Thm.~2.2]{SMKGS-spectre}. We say that the hierarchy \emph{covers the plane} if the supertiles
containing some (equivalently, every) tile exhaust the plane; equivalence holds because, once a supertile containing
one tile contains another, the two tiles have the same supertiles from that level on. If it does not, the tiling splits into two or more infinite supertiles
(unions of nested chains) with disjoint interiors, separated by a \emph{fault}. (Each tile lies in exactly one
supertile of each level, so two such unions are equal or disjoint.)

\begin{mainthm}[The Daedalus Theorem]\label{thm:A}
In every homochiral tiling of the plane by $\Tile$ whose supertile hierarchy covers the plane, with every tile carrying
the decoration of \cref{fig:tile}, there is no closed strand: every strand is a two-way infinite path of ribbon pieces.
\end{mainthm}

Our second result is the single strand. We prove it for one specific tiling, the \emph{nest tiling} $\N$, which is
grown from a single tile $N_0$ by repeatedly taking the $\Delta$-supertile $N_{b+1}$ that has $N_b$ as its child
number~$1$ (\cref{sec:nest}). In the usual language of substitution tilings, $\N$ is the tiling generated from one tile
as a seed by iterating the substitution about it, a fixed point of the substitution (in the combinatorial sense;
compare~\cite{BaakeGrimm}); in the tile addresses of~\cite[\S2.8]{TathamTransducers} (after~\cite{GoodmanStrauss1999}), $N_0$
has the address $\Delta\xleftarrow{1}\Delta\xleftarrow{1}\Delta\xleftarrow{1}\cdots$, a $\Delta$ at every level, each child~$1$ of the next. \Cref{fig:tile,fig:merge,fig:skeleton,fig:nest} show blocks of it.

\begin{mainthm}[The Ariadne Theorem]\label{thm:B}
In the nest tiling $\N$, with every tile carrying the decoration of \cref{fig:tile}, all the ribbon pieces of all the
tiles form a single strand. It is a two-way infinite path
that passes through every tile. More precisely, for every $b\ge 1$, all the ribbon arcs of the block $N_b$ lie on a
single arc of $N_{b+4}$, and of $N_{b+3}$ when $b$ is even.
\end{mainthm}

\Cref{fig:merge} shows the merging for $N_0$: its fourteen pieces lie on $5$, $2$ and $1$ arcs of $N_1$, $N_2$ and
$N_3$. Since the curves cross only transversally and in pairs, over and under can be assigned alternately, as in
Celtic knotwork (\cref{rem:overunder}); the single strand then becomes an alternating diagram of a line.

\subsection*{The method}
The proof has three ideas.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item \emph{Black boxes.} Seen from outside, a supertile is just a list of which ribbon end on its boundary is
joined to which. We show that every supertile of every kind and level has the same \emph{signature}: a hook at every
boundary vertex but two adjacent ones, $A$ and $B$, where the two ribbons cross over; and no closed strand inside
(\cref{def:signature}, \cref{thm:signature}).
\item \emph{Zigzags.} When two such boxes are glued along a common boundary path, the ribbons zigzag along it, and
the outcome depends only on the parity of its length (\cref{lem:zigzag}). So lengthening a shared path by two edges
changes nothing (\cref{lem:lengthen}).
\item \emph{Fixed parities.} The supertiles are combinatorially hexagons and compose by the substitution
of~\cite[Fig.~5.1]{SMKGS-spectre}. Their side lengths are given by Fibonacci numbers (\cref{prop:fib}); they only grow
with the level, and their parities never change. So the side lengths at any level are reached from those at level~$1$
by lengthening, two edges at a time (\cref{lem:reach}), and a finite computation at level~$1$ gives the signature at
every level.
\end{enumerate}
The merging of all ribbons into one strand (\cref{thm:merge}) follows the same pattern. Where the ribbons of a block
go one level up depends only on where they leave it, for most of its rim only on the side (\cref{lem:junctionmap});
this map is computed at level~$1$ and carried to every level by the same lengthening. Followed three or four levels
up, it puts every arc of a block on one strand.

\subsection*{Computer assistance}
The proof uses computer checks of finitely many explicit facts: the signature of the Mystic and the level-$1$ data
(vertex positions, side lengths, the position of $A$, and the signature of the level-$1$ supertiles), the agreement of
our supertile data with~\cite[Figs.~4.1, 5.1]{SMKGS-spectre}, and two computations on the $18$ compositions of
level-$1$ supertiles, built from the tables and the level-$1$ side lengths alone: their signature
(\cref{comp:compose}) and, for $\Delta$, the junction map (\cref{comp:junction}). The geometric computations (the
Mystic and level-$1$ data, and the composition pattern for $m\le3$) were carried out twice, by two separate programs:
once in JavaScript with floating-point coordinates, and once in Python in exact arithmetic, with coordinates in
$\ZZ[e^{i\pi/6}]$. The computations on the compositions are exact computations with integers, done by a Python
program and again in the kernel of the Lean proof assistant. We also checked the conclusions directly on the actual
supertiles up to level~$9$ (about $1.3\times 10^8$ tiles), and by a second route, through skeletons and a symbolic
computation with certified inequalities, which the proof does not use (\cref{rem:skeleton,rem:labels}).
\Cref{app:computations} lists every computation and the script that performs it. The programs, with logs of a full
run, are in the ancillary files of this paper (folder \code{code/}).

\subsection*{Scope}
\Cref{thm:A} covers every tiling whose hierarchy covers the plane. \Cref{thm:B} is proved for the nest tiling only,
and we make no claim about the number of strands in any other tiling. The single strand is not a property of every
such tiling: computations indicate that in at least one other tiling whose hierarchy covers the plane the pieces form
exactly three strands (\cref{rem:three}). Tilings with faults are not covered by either theorem; see
\cref{sec:scope}.

\subsection*{Related work}
Curves drawn on tiles and joined across edges appear in several settings; in all of them each curve is closed or
two-way infinite, as in \cref{lem:degree2}. For Truchet tilings by squares carrying two quarter-circle arcs, the
closed curves of random tilings have been studied by percolation and renormalisation methods~\cite{Pete2008,Hooper2011}.
On rectangular grids, the mirror curves behind Celtic, Tchokwe and Tamil designs have a computable number of
components; without internal mirrors it is the greatest common divisor of the side lengths~\cite{JablanKnotsArt}. On
aperiodic tilings, the arc decoration of the Penrose rhombs due to Conway and Penrose gives closed curves apart from
at most two that cross the whole tiling (see~\cite{FlickerSimonParameswaran} and the references there), and Girih
interlace drawn on Penrose tilings appears to consist of closed strands of unbounded variety~\cite{Cline2024}. Single
curves through all the tiles of finite patches have been built as Hamiltonian cycles on Ammann--Beenker
tilings~\cite{SinghLloydFlicker}, and as space-filling self-similar curves from decorations of aperiodic substitution
tilings~\cite{Pautze2021}. Closest to the present work, an online project~\cite{BohemianMiserSpectre} decorates
selected edges of $\Tile$ and describes, in one nested tiling, a single non-crossing curve that visits every tile once,
with a machine check of its combinatorial core. That curve uses only some of the edges and has no crossings. Here every
edge of every tile carries a ribbon end, the pieces cross, and the single strand is a knot diagram in the sense of
Celtic knotwork. We are not aware of earlier work on such full knotwork decorations that form a single strand.

\subsection*{Use of AI and verification status}
This work is an experiment in using AI for mathematical exploration. The author came up with the original idea of
looking for a mono-stranded aperiodic knot, and then used an AI system (Claude, by Anthropic) to search for it; from the
$34$ candidates found, the author chose design $0.1{:}01$ and drew its ribbons by hand. From then on the author and the
AI system worked together: the AI system did the detailed work (the programs, the proof, the Lean formalisation and
this paper), and the author directed, guided and corrected it. Lean checks the combinatorial core of the proof; the
link with actual tilings is argued in words. The author has read parts of this paper and spot-checked figures and
counts; most of the proof has not been reviewed by a human, and no professional mathematician has reviewed it. The AI
system reviewed its own argument in several passes. A program or check called \emph{independent} or \emph{separate}
here is a separate implementation by the same AI system. A fuller account:
\url{https://cortado-j.github.io/SPECTRE_KNOT/made.html}.

\subsection*{Formal verification}
The combinatorial part of the proof is formalised in the Lean~4 proof assistant~\cite{Lean4}, using its core library
only (folder \code{lean/} of the ancillary files). Lean's kernel has checked the following, in a combinatorial form in
which a patch, or a whole tiling, is described by which edge of which tile is glued to which edge of which other tile:
every supertile of every kind and every level $m\ge1$ has the signature (\cref{thm:signature}), and so contains no
closed strand; in the union of any chain of supertiles, each a child of the next, in which every edge is eventually
glued, every strand is a two-way infinite path (\cref{thm:A}), and the nest is such a chain; in the nest tiling, built
as the union of the blocks $N_b$, the pieces form one two-way infinite strand (\cref{thm:B}), with the bounds of
\cref{thm:merge}, which are exact (the last two rows of \cref{tab:merge}); and every vertex of every tile of $N_b$ is
surrounded by tiles of $N_{b+2}$ (\cref{lem:gap}(a), at the vertices of tiles). The kernel also re-runs the finite
computations that these use (\cref{comp:compose,comp:base,comp:junction}). The inputs are
\cref{tab:children,tab:sides,tab:psides,tab:letters}, as computed from the geometry, and the nine level-$1$ supertiles as
glued patches of tiles, computed in exact arithmetic by the program of \cref{app:computations}. The link with tilings
of the plane is not formalised; it is a stated assumption of the formal statements, in three parts: that the
supertiles of an actual tiling are glued as these tables say, which rests on~\cite{SMKGS-spectre} and
\cref{sec:spectre} (\cref{thm:smkgs}, \cref{prop:pattern,lem:rigid}); the first step of the proof of \cref{thm:A}, that
a tiling whose hierarchy covers the plane is the union of such a chain; and the geometric
\cref{prop:nestwell,prop:nesttiling}, with the step of \cref{lem:gap}(a) from the vertices of tiles to the points of the
plane. The Mystic
(\cref{lem:tilesig}) is not treated separately: the formalisation starts from the level-$1$ supertiles. The formal proofs follow
\cref{sec:decoration,sec:boxes,sec:reduce,sec:lengths,sec:induction,sec:nest,sec:strand}, with these differences of detail: strands
are modelled by two involutions on ports (\cref{lem:degree2}); the formal proof of \cref{lem:lengthen} shows that a move creates
no closed strand through \cref{prop:reduce}(b),(c), rather than directly; \cref{prop:reduce}(c) is proved in the form used, that if every
reduced walk reaches the rim there is no closed strand; in \cref{comp:junction} the kernel computes the second part,
and the first is proved for every level from the signature of child~$1$; \cref{lem:junctions} is proved with two
edges from the ends of a side, which is what its use needs; \cref{lem:gap}(b) is proved from the tables, as its proof
notes; and the proof of \cref{lem:gap}(a) at the vertices of tiles uses that every supertile is a disc at its rim,
which the paper takes from the geometry and the formalisation proves level by level. The proofs use only
Lean's standard axioms. The formalisation was written by the AI system as well; its statement file
(\code{lean/SpectreKnot/Statement.lean}) contains every definition that the formal theorems depend on, with comments in
plain English.

\section{Spectre tilings}\label{sec:spectre}

\subsection{The tile}
Let $\rho=e^{i\pi/6}$. The polygon $\Tile$ has fourteen edges of unit length; we label its vertices
$v_0,\dots,v_{13}$ counter-clockwise and its edges $e_i=v_iv_{i+1}$ (indices mod $14$), with $v_0=0$ and
\[
v_{i+1}-v_i=\rho^{k_i},\qquad (k_0,\dots,k_{13})=(0,10,1,3,0,2,5,7,4,6,6,8,11,9).
\]
All vertices lie in $\ZZ[\rho]$, and the edge directions are multiples of $30^\circ$; see \cref{fig:tileports}.
This is the equilateral member $\Tile$ of the family $\mathrm{Tile}(a,b)$~\cite[\S\S1--2]{SMKGS-spectre}; as there,
its long side is split into the two collinear edges $e_9,e_{10}$, so that $v_{10}$ has interior angle $180^\circ$.
Our $\Tile$ is the mirror image of the polygon drawn in~\cite[Fig.~1.1]{SMKGS-spectre}; it has the handedness of the
single Spectre and of the Mystic drawn in~\cite[Fig.~2.1]{SMKGS-spectre}.
\emph{Spectres} are tiles, obtained for instance by replacing every edge of $\Tile$ by a suitable curve, whose tilings
correspond exactly to the homochiral tilings by $\Tile$~\cite[\S2, Lemma~2.1]{SMKGS-spectre}. We work throughout with
$\Tile$ and \emph{homochiral} tilings, in which any two tiles are related by a rotation and a translation; reflecting
the whole plane (with the decoration below, which maps strands to strands) if necessary, we assume that every tile is the image of $\Tile$ under a rotation and a translation.
Since the correspondence keeps the isometries $g_i$~\cite[\S2]{SMKGS-spectre}, and~\cite[p.~5]{SMKGS-spectre} treats the
two as the same universe of tilings, what~\cite{SMKGS-spectre} proves for tilings by Spectres holds for homochiral
tilings by $\Tile$, and we use it in that form. Such tilings exist~\cite[Thm.~2.2]{SMKGS-spectre}, and every tiling by $\Tile$ is vertex to vertex, so tiles meet full edge
to full edge~\cite[proof of Lemma~2.1]{SMKGS-spectre}. The tile has no non-trivial symmetry, proper or improper (its cyclic sequence of edge directions
has none), so an isometry between two tiles is unique.

\subsection{Supertiles}
We use the hierarchy of~\cite{SMKGS-spectre}. There are nine \emph{kinds}
\[
\K=\{\Gamma,\Delta,\Theta,\Lambda,\Xi,\Pi,\Sigma,\Phi,\Psi\}.
\]
A \emph{level-$0$ supertile} of kind $K\neq\Gamma$ is a single tile; a level-$0$ supertile of kind $\Gamma$ is a
\emph{Mystic}~\cite[Fig.~2.1]{SMKGS-spectre}, the pair of tiles $\{\Tile,\ g(\Tile)\}$ with $g(z)=\rho z+v_8$, and
its congruent copies; we call $\Tile$ its \emph{first} tile. (The outline of the Mystic, which~\cite{SMKGS-spectre} calls
symmetric, has a mirror symmetry, but the Mystic has no non-trivial proper symmetry: a proper
symmetry exchanging its tiles would be an involution taking $\Tile$ to $g(\Tile)$, but the only proper motion doing
so is $g$, a rotation by $30^\circ$. So the first tile is determined by the pair.) A level-$(m+1)$ supertile is the union of level-$m$ supertiles, its \emph{children}, arranged in a fixed way:
eight children, or seven for kind $\Gamma$, of the kinds listed in \cref{tab:children}. A level-$1$ supertile has
the kind of its cluster in~\cite[Fig.~4.1]{SMKGS-spectre}, which is determined by the positions of its six vertices
(\cref{sec:own})~\cite[proof of Thm.~4.1]{SMKGS-spectre}; a level-$(m+1)$ supertile, $m\ge1$, has kind $T$ if its
children form the panel $T$ of~\cite[Fig.~5.1]{SMKGS-spectre}. For $m\ge1$, child $c$ is the child at the position in
the last row of \cref{tab:children}; at level $1$ the children are numbered as in \cref{fig:level1}. (This is the order
used by Kaplan's implementation of the key-point construction of~\cite[App.~A]{SMKGS-spectre},
see~\cite{KaplanSpectrePage}. That implementation gives \cref{tab:children} and, tile for tile, the same supertiles as
\code{exact/}\allowbreak\code{exact.py} up to level~$4$; see \code{crosscheck/}\allowbreak\code{kaplan\_compare.py}.) For
definiteness a single tile gets the kind of its position in its level-$1$ supertile; only the distinction between
$\Gamma$ and the other kinds is used at level~$0$.
In particular child~$1$ of every supertile is a $\Delta$, and the last child is a $\Gamma$; at levels $m\ge1$ a $\Delta$
occurs only as child~$1$.

The nine level-$1$ supertiles are the Spectre clusters ($9$ tiles) and the Mystic cluster ($8$ tiles, kind $\Gamma$)

The following theorem restates, in our language, results of~\cite{SMKGS-spectre}.

\begin{theorem}[Smith, Myers, Kaplan, Goodman-Strauss~\cite{SMKGS-spectre}]\label{thm:smkgs}
Let $\T$ be a homochiral tiling by $\Tile$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\alph*)}]
\item \textup{\cite[Thms.~2.2, 3.1, 4.1, \S5, Fig.~2.1]{SMKGS-spectre}} The tiles of $\T$ group uniquely into level-$1$
supertiles. Each contains one Mystic, in the position shown in~\cite[Fig.~2.1]{SMKGS-spectre}; this Mystic and the other
tiles, taken singly, are the level-$0$ supertiles inside it. For every $m\ge1$ the level-$m$ supertiles of $\T$ group
uniquely into level-$(m+1)$ supertiles. Each tile lies in a unique nested sequence $X_0\subset X_1\subset\cdots$ with
$X_m$ a level-$m$ supertile.
\item \textup{\cite[Thms.~3.1, 4.1, 5.1, \S5]{SMKGS-spectre}} For every $m\ge 1$, the tiling of the plane by the level-$m$
supertiles of $\T$ is combinatorially equivalent (homeomorphic as a topological complex~\cite[\S2.2]{SMKGS-spectre}) to a
tiling by regular hexagons, each level-$m$ supertile of kind $K$
corresponding to a hexagon marked as the hexagon $K$ of~\cite[Fig.~4.2]{SMKGS-spectre} for even $m$, and as its
mirror image for odd $m$, adjacent supertiles corresponding to hexagons sharing an edge with matching labels; and the
grouping into level-$(m+1)$ supertiles corresponds, up to reflection, to the grouping of hexagons shown
in~\cite[Fig.~5.1]{SMKGS-spectre}.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

In (a), \cite[Thm.~4.1]{SMKGS-spectre} gives the grouping into clusters; its uniqueness, like that of the higher
levels, is part of~\cite[Thm.~2.2]{SMKGS-spectre}, and we use it only to speak of \emph{the} hierarchy of $\T$. In (b),
the handedness is that of our level-$1$ supertiles, which are the mirror images of the clusters drawn
in~\cite[Figs.~2.1, 4.1]{SMKGS-spectre} (see the base case of ($\ast$) in the proof of \cref{prop:pattern}); it
alternates with $m$ by~\cite[Thm.~5.1]{SMKGS-spectre}.

In particular every level-$m$ supertile ($m\ge1$) is a closed topological disc whose boundary, its \emph{rim}, is a
simple closed polygon made of tile edges. Its \emph{vertices} are the six points of the rim where it meets two other
level-$m$ supertiles, and its \emph{sides} are the six arcs of the rim between consecutive vertices. (The tile
vertices on a rim are called \emph{rim vertices}; for a supertile of level $\ge1$, ``vertex'' alone always means one of
the six.) A vertex of one
supertile that lies on the rim of another is a vertex of that one too, since exactly three supertiles meet there. Each side is
shared, as a path of tile edges, with exactly one neighbouring supertile.

\subsection{Own orientation, side numbering and the crossover corner}\label{sec:own}
By~\cite[Thm.~5.1]{SMKGS-spectre}, the supertiles composed from a tiling by marked hexagons are combinatorially
equivalent to the \emph{mirror images} of those hexagons. So, at each level, the cyclic order of the labelled sides is
reversed, and it is convenient to orient supertiles alternately.

\begin{definition}[Own orientation]\label{def:own}
The \emph{own orientation} of a level-$m$ supertile is counter-clockwise if $m$ is even and clockwise if $m$ is odd.
We write $\omega_m=(-1)^m$.
\end{definition}

Thus a child and its parent are always oriented oppositely. Each supertile has a distinguished rim vertex $A$, its
\emph{crossover corner}, defined recursively: for a tile, $A=v_0$; for a Mystic, $A$ is the vertex $v_0$ of its first
tile $\Tile$; for a level-$(m+1)$ supertile, $A$ is the crossover corner of its $\Gamma$ child. (That this point lies
on the rim is computed for levels $0$ and $1$ and follows from \cref{prop:pattern}(d) at higher levels.) The second corner $B$, adjacent to $A$, appears in \cref{thm:signature};
we call $A$ and $B$ the \emph{crossover corners}. The vertices of a level-$m$ supertile ($m\ge1$) are numbered
$0,\dots,5$ in own order, starting with the first vertex at or after $A$; side $j$ runs from vertex $j$ to vertex
$j+1$. \Cref{fig:level1} shows this numbering for the level-$1$ supertiles.

\subsection{The composition pattern}\label{sec:pattern}

in our numbering.

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]

\begin{proposition}[The pattern holds at every level]\label{prop:pattern}
Let $\T$ be a homochiral tiling by $\Tile$, let $m\ge1$, and let $Y$ be a level-$(m+1)$ supertile of $\T$ of kind
$T$, with children $X_0,\dots,X_{n_T-1}$. Then:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\alph*)}]
\item $X_c$ has kind $K_c$ (\cref{tab:children});
\item side $j$ of $X_c$ is the same path of tile edges as side $j'$ of $X_{c'}$, traversed in the opposite direction,
whenever \cref{tab:sides} lists $c'.j'$, and it lies on the rim of $Y$ when the table lists $\rim$;
\item side $J$ of $Y$ is the concatenation of the children's sides listed in \cref{tab:psides}, and vertex $J$ of $Y$
is the vertex $j+1$ of the child $c$ whose side $c.j$ is listed first;
\item the crossover corner $A$ of $Y$ is vertex $0$ of its $\Gamma$ child, which is vertex $0$ of $Y$ if $T\neq\Sigma$, and
lies inside side $5$ of $Y$ if $T=\Sigma$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Justification]
By \cref{thm:smkgs}(b), the level-$m$ supertiles inside $Y$ are combinatorially the hexagons of the corresponding
panel of~\cite[Fig.~5.1]{SMKGS-spectre}: hexagon edges correspond to sides and hexagon vertices to vertices. We use
the following dictionary between the labels of~\cite[Fig.~4.2]{SMKGS-spectre} and our side numbers:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item[($\ast$)] side $j$ of a level-$m$ supertile of kind $K$ carries the label of the edge of hexagon $K$ in direction
W, SW, SE, E, NE, NW for $j=0,\dots,5$ respectively (\cref{tab:letters}); its crossover corner $A$ is vertex~$0$ if
$K\neq\Sigma$; if $K=\Sigma$, $A$ is not a vertex, so it lies in the relative interior of side~$5$.
\end{itemize}
We show ($\ast$) by induction on $m$. For $m=1$ it is a computation on the level-$1$ supertiles, which have
$E_1=44$ rim edges for $\Gamma$ and $46$ for the other kinds; their vertices lie at the rim positions, counted from
$A$ in own order,
\[
\begin{array}{lll}
\Gamma: 0,7,14,24,34,39 & \Delta: 0,10,15,23,30,40 & \Theta: 0,10,15,26,31,41\\
\Lambda: 0,10,15,23,30,41 & \Xi: 0,7,15,26,31,41 & \Pi: 0,7,15,23,30,41\\
\Sigma: 4,10,15,23,30,40 & \Phi: 0,10,15,23,31,41 & \Psi: 0,7,15,23,31,41.
\end{array}
\]
Drawn with the edges in odd directions lengthened to $\sqrt3$, which turns the tiles into hats and
turtles~\cite[\S3]{SMKGS-spectre}, and in the handedness of~\cite[Fig.~4.1]{SMKGS-spectre}, the nine level-$1$
supertiles coincide with the nine marked clusters there: the same outlines, the vertices at the marked dots, and the
labels of \cref{tab:letters} in the places where that figure has them; $A$ is the dot between the sides labelled W and NW
(for $\Sigma$, a point of the NW side), on the light tile of the Mystic. A script checks this against a render of the
figure (\cref{app:computations}): for each cluster it finds the six dots, fits our six vertices to them by a proper
similarity with residual below $1\%$ of an edge (any other matching, or the vertices of any other kind, fits at least
$40$ times worse), finds our whole rim on the printed outline and our first Mystic tile on the lighter shaded tile, and
compares the labels, read from the figure by hand, with \cref{tab:letters}. Suppose ($\ast$) holds for the children of $Y$. The panel does not label the inner
hexagons, but the matching rules fix the rotation of each of them, and there is exactly one consistent choice (it is
found by the script of \cref{rem:patterncheck}). With this dictionary, \cref{tab:sides,tab:psides} are then a
transcription of the panel of~\cite[Fig.~5.1]{SMKGS-spectre} for $T$, including its vertices (the six marked points
on its outline); this gives (a)--(c). The crossover corner of $Y$ is that of its $\Gamma$ child, which is the child's
vertex~$0$ by ($\ast$), and \cref{tab:psides} places this vertex at the start of the parent's side~$0$ if $T\neq\Sigma$
and inside side~$5$ if $T=\Sigma$; this is (d). Finally, the labels that the panel assigns to the parent's sides,
numbered from this point in the parent's own order, are those of \cref{tab:letters} for every $T$. So ($\ast$) holds
for $Y$. In particular $A$ always lies on the rim.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Computer cross-checks of the pattern]\label{rem:patterncheck}
Since \cref{tab:sides,tab:psides} carry the whole weight of the citation, we checked them in two independent ways.
First, we built the actual supertiles of levels $1$ to $8$ with the key-point construction of~\cite[App.~A]{SMKGS-spectre}
and extracted the pattern of each composition $m\to m+1$ for $m=1,\dots,7$. For $m\le4$ the vertices were found as the points where three level-$m$ supertiles meet, observed inside larger
supertiles. For $m=5,\dots,8$ they were propagated by the rule of \cref{prop:pattern}(c), learnt at the observed level
of the same parity; at levels $5$ and $6$ the propagated vertices agree with the observed ones (for $\Psi$, with the
four of its six vertices that were observed). So for $m=6,7$ the rule (c) is an input of the extraction rather than
something it tests. All seven extracted patterns are identical to each other and
to \cref{tab:sides,tab:psides}; for $m\le3$ this was done in exact arithmetic (\cref{app:computations}). Second, the
nine panels of~\cite[Fig.~5.1]{SMKGS-spectre} were encoded: kinds, positions and edge labels by hand, and the marked
vertices (with the centre of each panel's $\Gamma$ hexagon) by a script from a render of the figure, which re-runs from
the PDF. A script then confirmed, for all nine kinds, that the encoding agrees with \cref{tab:sides,tab:psides}, and
that the side-to-label dictionary above is the same in all $71$ placements of a child. Two deliberately corrupted encodings (two outer labels
exchanged; one wrong label in~\cite[Fig.~4.2]{SMKGS-spectre}) were rejected. We also checked that the tiles of every supertile of levels $1$ to $7$ (up to $2{,}147{,}679$ tiles) have
disjoint interiors and exactly fill its rim.
\end{remark}

We shall use one consequence of \cref{thm:smkgs}(a) and \cref{prop:pattern} repeatedly.

\begin{lemma}[Rigidity]\label{lem:rigid}
Any two level-$m$ supertiles of the same kind, in possibly different homochiral tilings, are related by a rotation
and translation that maps descendants to descendants with their numbers (children, their children, and so on, down
to the tiles), and it is the only such motion. For $m\ge1$ it maps the six vertices, the sides and the corner $A$ of one onto those of the other.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Induction on $m$. For $m=0$ this is clear, since the Mystic is a fixed pair of tiles without proper symmetry. For
$m=1$, the level-$1$ supertiles of a homochiral tiling are congruent copies of the clusters of
\cref{fig:level1}~\cite[Fig.~2.1, \S4]{SMKGS-spectre}, with their vertices at the points marked
in~\cite[Fig.~4.1]{SMKGS-spectre}; the numbers of their children are those of the reference clusters of
\cref{fig:level1}, and $A$ is fixed by the Mystic. Let $m\ge1$. By induction, the children of two level-$(m+1)$
supertiles $Y,Y'$ of kind $T$ are pairwise related by unique proper motions $g_c$, which map the vertices of child $c$
of $Y$ onto those of child $c$ of $Y'$ with their numbers. If side $j$ of child $c$ is glued to side $j'$ of child
$c'$, then by \cref{prop:pattern}(b), applied to $Y$ and to $Y'$, vertices $j$ and $j+1$ of $c$ coincide with vertices
$j'+1$ and $j'$ of $c'$, in $Y$ and in $Y'$. So $g_c$ and $g_{c'}$ agree on two distinct points; being proper motions,
they are equal. The children's adjacency graph is connected, so all the $g_c$ are one motion $g$. By
\cref{prop:pattern}(c),(d), the vertices and the corner $A$ of $Y$ are vertices and the corner $A$ of children, so $g$
maps them, and hence the sides, onto those of $Y'$. Since $g$ agrees with each $g_c$, it maps descendants to
descendants with their numbers. Any proper motion that does so maps the first tile of the Mystic at the bottom of the
chain of last ($\Gamma$) children to its counterpart, so it equals $g$, since a tile has no symmetry.
\end{proof}

Write $E_m(K)$ for the number of rim edges (equally, rim vertices) of a level-$m$ supertile of kind $K$, and
$\ell_m(K,j)$ for the number of edges of its side $j$. By \cref{lem:rigid} these numbers are well defined.

\section{The decoration and its strands}\label{sec:decoration}

\subsection{Ports and pieces}
Let $t$ be a tile and $e$ one of its edges, with endpoints $x$ and $y$. The tile has two \emph{ports} on $e$, the
port \emph{near $x$} and the port \emph{near $y$}; formally, they are the pairs $(e,x)$ and $(e,y)$. In the drawing
(\cref{fig:tileports}), $e$ carries one crossing point, and the two ribbon ends of $t$ that meet it, one running off
towards $x$ and one towards $y$, are these two ports. For a vertex $v_i$ of the tile, write
\[
\Rp(v_i)=\text{the port on } e_i \text{ near } v_i,\qquad \Lp(v_i)=\text{the port on } e_{i-1} \text{ near } v_i ,
\]
the ports just after and just before the corner $v_i$ in counter-clockwise order. Each of the $28$ ports is
$\Rp(v_i)$ or $\Lp(v_i)$ for exactly one $i$.

\begin{definition}[The decoration]\label{def:decoration}
The \emph{decoration} joins the $28$ ports of $\Tile$ in pairs by fourteen \emph{pieces}:
\[
\underbrace{\Lp(v_i)\leftrightarrow\Rp(v_i)\quad(2\le i\le 13)}_{\text{twelve hooks}},\qquad
\underbrace{\Rp(v_0)\leftrightarrow\Rp(v_1),\qquad \Lp(v_0)\leftrightarrow\Lp(v_1)}_{\text{the crossover}}.
\]
Every tile of a homochiral tiling carries the image of this decoration.
\end{definition}

In our drawing the two crossover pieces cross exactly once, transversally, and this is the only crossing inside the
tile (\cref{rem:overunder}). Each hook turns
back round one vertex. \Cref{fig:tileports} shows the pieces and their ports, and \cref{fig:tile} the ribbons as drawn. The drawing uses cubic
B\'ezier curves, with the crossing point of edge $e_i$ at distance $0.5+0.15(-1)^i$ from $v_i$. Only the pairing of
\cref{def:decoration} matters for the theorems; any drawing that realises it may be used.

\subsection{The straight-through rule}
Let $\T$ be a homochiral tiling, and let $e$ be an edge shared by tiles $t$ and $t'$, with endpoints $x$ and $y$.

\begin{definition}[Straight-through rule]\label{def:rule}
The port of $t$ on $e$ near $x$ is \emph{joined} to the port of $t'$ on $e$ near $y$, and the port of $t$ near $y$ to
the port of $t'$ near $x$.
\end{definition}

This is what a ribbon does when it continues straight through the crossing point (\cref{fig:rule}). In the drawing
the two tiles' crossing points on $e$ coincide. The reason is that tiles in a homochiral tiling traverse a shared edge
in opposite directions, and an edge $e_i$ of one tile is always glued to an edge $e_j$ of the other with $i+j$ odd;
this follows from the vertex colouring of~\cite[proof of Lemma~2.1]{SMKGS-spectre}: the interior angles of $\Tile$
alternate between multiples of $90^\circ$ and of $120^\circ$, and these classes must agree where vertices meet. As
$v_i$ of one tile meets $v_{j+1}$ of the other, $i$ and $j+1$ have the same parity. We verified it on all supertiles up to level~$4$.
We also checked that the drawn ribbons realise the pairing on all supertiles up to level~$3$
(\cref{app:computations}). The proofs use only \cref{def:decoration,def:rule}.

\subsection{Strands}
\begin{definition}[Strand graph]\label{def:strandgraph}
Let $\T$ be a homochiral tiling. The \emph{strand graph} $\G(\T)$ is the multigraph with one vertex for each piece of
each tile and one edge for each pair of ports joined by the straight-through rule, between the two pieces having
these ports as ends. A \emph{strand} is a connected component of $\G(\T)$; a strand is \emph{closed} if it is finite.
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:degree2}
Every vertex of $\G(\T)$ has degree $2$. Hence every strand is either a finite cycle (a closed strand) or a two-way
infinite path.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
A piece has two ends, each a port of its tile on some edge $e$. In a tiling of the plane, $e$ is shared by exactly one
other tile, so each end is joined to exactly one port, an end of a piece of that neighbour. Joins always link pieces of
different tiles, so $\G(\T)$ has no loops, and every vertex has degree exactly $2$. A connected, locally finite
multigraph with all degrees $2$ is a finite cycle (possibly of length $2$) or a two-way infinite path.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[A single strand needs a non-periodic tiling]\label{rem:periodic}
Let $\T'$ be any edge-to-edge tiling of the plane by compact decorated tiles (not necessarily copies of $\Tile$),
each carrying at least one piece, with one crossing point on each edge, and with strands defined as above by the
straight-through rule. Suppose that $\T'$, decoration
included, is invariant under a translation $\tau\ne0$, and that its strand graph is a single two-way infinite path,
with vertices $x\in\ZZ$. Then $\tau$ acts on the path by a graph automorphism, and freely, since a non-zero translation
moves every tile. An automorphism of the path is $x\mapsto x+k$ or $x\mapsto c-x$. A reflection $x\mapsto c-x$
satisfies $\tau^2=\mathrm{id}$ on the path, but $\tau^2$ is again a non-zero translation and fixes no piece; so $\tau$
is $x\mapsto x+k$ with $k\ne0$, and the pieces fall into $|k|$ orbits under $\langle\tau\rangle$. But the tiles fall
into infinitely many orbits: the images of one tile from each orbit cover the cylinder $\RR^2/\langle\tau\rangle$,
which has infinite area. Since every tile has a piece, the pieces also fall into infinitely many orbits. This contradiction shows that the ribbons of a decorated tiling with a translational
symmetry never form a single strand. Every tiling by $\Tile$ of one handedness is
non-periodic~\cite[Thm.~2.2]{SMKGS-spectre}, so this obstruction does not arise there.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Over and under]\label{rem:overunder}
Draw the pieces of every tile as in \cref{fig:tile}. We checked numerically that each piece stays inside its tile away
from its ends, that the two crossover pieces cross exactly once, transversally (at about $90^\circ$), and that no other two pieces of a
tile meet except at their common ends on the edges; and that at each crossing point the two pieces of a tile meet the
edge at $\pm45^\circ$ (\cref{app:computations}). So a ribbon
continues smoothly across an edge, and the curves in the plane cross only transversally, in pairs: once inside each
tile and at right angles at each edge. Colour each region of the complement of the curves by the parity of the number
of crossings of a generic path from a fixed region. This is well defined: the parity of the number of crossings of a
generic closed loop does not change when the loop is moved across an arc of the curves, or across a crossing point,
where four arcs meet; and every closed loop in the plane can be shrunk to a point. Adjacent regions get different
colours. The usual checkerboard rule then makes over and under alternate along every strand~\cite{CromwellKnots}. With
such a choice the single strand of \cref{thm:B} becomes an alternating diagram of a line, as in Celtic knotwork. None
of this affects which pieces lie on which strand.
\end{remark}

\section{Boxes and the signature}\label{sec:boxes}

\subsection{Patches and boxes}
A \emph{patch} is a finite set $P$ of tiles of a homochiral tiling whose union is a closed disc with a simple polygonal
boundary, the \emph{rim} $\rim P$. Every supertile of level $\ge1$ is a patch (\cref{thm:smkgs}), and so are a tile and a Mystic (whose two tiles
share four consecutive edges, $e_6,\dots,e_9$ of the first tile; its rim has $20$ edges). Every rim edge
belongs to exactly one tile of $P$. Let $E=E(P)$ be the number of rim edges, and number the rim vertices
$u_0,\dots,u_{E-1}$ in a chosen orientation of the rim, indices mod $E$. For a supertile we always use its own
orientation (\cref{def:own}). For a rim vertex $u_k$ define the \emph{rim ports}
\[
\Rp(u_k)=\text{the port on } u_ku_{k+1} \text{ near } u_k,\qquad \Lp(u_k)=\text{the port on } u_{k-1}u_k \text{ near } u_k,
\]
where the port belongs to the tile of $P$ containing that edge. There are $2E$ rim ports. For the tile itself, with
its counter-clockwise orientation, these are the ports $\Rp(v_i),\Lp(v_i)$ of \cref{sec:decoration}. Reversing the
orientation exchanges $\Rp$ and $\Lp$ at every vertex.

\begin{lemma}[The box of a patch]\label{lem:box}
Start at a rim port of $P$ and follow the pieces of the tiles of $P$, crossing every interior edge of $P$ by the
straight-through rule. The path ends at a different rim port. This defines an involution $\pi_P$ on the rim ports
without fixed points. The pieces of $P$ that are not reached from any rim port form finitely many cycles, the
\emph{closed strands of $P$}; write $\kappa(P)$ for their number.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The pieces of $P$ and the joins across interior edges form a finite graph in which every piece has degree $2$, except
that a piece end at a rim port has no partner. Its components are therefore paths, whose two ends are rim ports, and
cycles. Paths have two distinct ends.
\end{proof}

We call the paths of \cref{lem:box} the \emph{arcs} of $P$; there are exactly $E$ of them. The triple $(\rim P,\pi_P,\kappa(P))$ is the \emph{box} of $P$. Everything we need about the inside of $P$ is in its box, as the next
lemma shows.

\begin{lemma}[Gluing]\label{lem:gluing}
Let a patch $P$ be the union of patches $P_1,\dots,P_n$ with pairwise disjoint interiors. Then the box of $P$ is
determined by the boxes of the $P_i$ together with the identification of their shared rim edges. More precisely,
$\pi_P$ is computed by alternately applying the $\pi_{P_i}$ and the straight-through rule across shared edges, until a
rim port of $P$ is reached. The closed strands of $P$ are those of the $P_i$ together with the cycles of this
alternation that contain no rim port of $P$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Every arc or closed strand of $P$ (\cref{lem:box}) is a concatenation of arcs and closed strands of the $P_i$, joined
across edges shared by two of the $P_i$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The signature}
\begin{definition}[Signature]\label{def:signature}
Let $A$ and $B$ be adjacent rim vertices of a patch $P$. We say that $P$ has the \emph{signature at $(A,B)$} if
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item $\pi_P(\Lp(u))=\Rp(u)$ for every rim vertex $u\notin\{A,B\}$ (a \emph{hook} at $u$);
\item $\pi_P(\Rp(A))=\Rp(B)$ and $\pi_P(\Lp(A))=\Lp(B)$ (the \emph{crossover});
\item $\kappa(P)=0$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

The definition is symmetric in $A$ and $B$ and does not depend on the orientation of the rim, since reversing it
exchanges $\Lp$ and $\Rp$. A patch with the signature has $E-2$ hook arcs, one at each rim vertex other than
$A$ and $B$, and two crossover arcs. \Cref{fig:signature} is a picture. By \cref{def:decoration}:

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:tilesig}
A tile has the signature at $(v_0,v_1)$, and a Mystic has the signature at the vertices $v_0,v_1$ of its first tile.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
For the tile this is \cref{def:decoration}. For the Mystic it is a finite computation with \cref{lem:gluing}:
there is no closed strand, the $18$ rim vertices other than these two are hooks, and the crossover persists
(\cref{app:computations}).
\end{proof}

\section{The Zigzag Lemma and the skeleton}\label{sec:reduce}

This section studies how the ribbons of glued boxes join. \Cref{sec:zigzag} proves the Zigzag Lemma, on which the
proofs in \cref{sec:induction,sec:strand} rest. \Cref{sec:skeleton} condenses a composition to its \emph{skeleton} and
follows ribbons on it (\cref{prop:reduce}); the Lean formalisation uses \cref{prop:reduce}, and the second routes of
\cref{rem:skeleton,rem:labels} use the skeleton.

Throughout this section a patch $P$ is the union of patches (\emph{children}) $X_0,\dots,X_{n-1}$ with disjoint
interiors, all of them oriented in the same rotational sense, and $P$ is oriented in that sense too. Each child $X_c$
has the signature at $(A_c,B_c)$. When $P$ is a supertile and the $X_c$ are its children, the children carry their
own orientation, so $P$ is oriented opposite to its own orientation. This does not matter for the signature, which
does not depend on the orientation (\cref{sec:boxes}); but it does exchange $\Lp$ and $\Rp$ at the parent's rim
vertices.

\subsection{Runs and the Zigzag Lemma}\label{sec:zigzag}
Two children $c\neq c'$ oriented in the same sense traverse a common rim path in opposite directions. For a rim vertex
$u$ of $X_c$ we write $\Rp_c(u),\Lp_c(u)$ for the rim ports of $X_c$ in the common orientation fixed above (for
supertiles, their own orientation).

\begin{lemma}[Zigzag Lemma]\label{lem:zigzag}
Let $u_0u_1\cdots u_n$ ($n\ge1$) be a path of $n$ edges lying on the rims of both $X_c$ and $X_{c'}$, traversed by $X_c$
from $u_0$ to $u_n$. Suppose that none of the interior vertices $u_1,\dots,u_{n-1}$ is a crossover corner of $X_c$ or of
$X_{c'}$. Then, alternately applying the straight-through rule across the edges $u_ku_{k+1}$ and the hooks of $X_c$ and
$X_{c'}$ at $u_1,\dots,u_{n-1}$, the ports at the two ends of the path are joined as follows:
\begin{align*}
n \text{ even:}\quad & \Rp_c(u_0)\leftrightarrow \Lp_c(u_n), && \Rp_{c'}(u_n)\leftrightarrow \Lp_{c'}(u_0);\\
n \text{ odd:}\quad & \Rp_c(u_0)\leftrightarrow \Rp_{c'}(u_n), && \Lp_c(u_n)\leftrightarrow \Lp_{c'}(u_0).
\end{align*}
These two connections use every port on the edges of the path, so no closed strand lies along the path. Moreover, for
$0<k<n$, the hook of $X_c$ at $u_k$ lies on the connection through $\Rp_c(u_0)$ if $k$ is even. If $k$ is odd, it lies on
the connection through $\Lp_c(u_n)$ when $n$ is odd, and on the connection through $\Rp_{c'}(u_n)$ when $n$ is even.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Start at $\Rp_c(u_0)$, the port of $X_c$ on $u_0u_1$ near $u_0$. The straight-through rule takes it to the port of
$X_{c'}$ on $u_0u_1$ near $u_1$. In the orientation of $X_{c'}$, which runs from $u_1$ to $u_0$ along this edge, that
port is $\Rp_{c'}(u_1)$. If $n=1$ we have reached $\Rp_{c'}(u_n)$. Otherwise $u_1$ is a hook of $X_{c'}$, which leads
to $\Lp_{c'}(u_1)$, the port of $X_{c'}$ on $u_1u_2$ near $u_1$. Crossing $u_1u_2$ leads to the port of $X_c$ on
$u_1u_2$ near $u_2$, which is $\Lp_c(u_2)$. If $n=2$ we stop. Otherwise the hook of $X_c$ at $u_2$ leads to $\Rp_c(u_2)$,
and the pattern repeats with all indices shifted by two. So the path visits the hooks of $X_{c'}$ at odd $k$ and the
hooks of $X_c$ at even $k$. It ends at $\Lp_c(u_n)$ if $n$ is even and at $\Rp_{c'}(u_n)$ if $n$ is odd. The same
argument from the other end, starting at $\Rp_{c'}(u_n)$ for even $n$ and at $\Lp_c(u_n)$ for odd $n$, gives the
second connection; it visits the remaining hooks. Each connection crosses each of the $n$ edges once, using two of its
four ports, so together they use all of them.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Marks, runs and the skeleton}\label{sec:skeleton}
\begin{definition}[Marks and runs]\label{def:marks}
A \emph{mark} is a point that is a rim vertex of some child and is either
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item a \emph{junction}: a rim vertex of a child $X_c$ at which the two rim edges of $X_c$ have different neighbours,
where a neighbour is a sibling or ``outside $P$''; or
\item one of the crossover corners $A_c$, $B_c$ of some child.
\end{enumerate}
A \emph{run} of $X_c$ is a path along the rim of $X_c$ between two consecutive marks lying on that rim. (In every
composition below each child has at least four marks on its rim, so runs are arcs with distinct ends.)
\end{definition}

Along a run the neighbour of $X_c$ does not change. So a run either lies on the rim of $P$ (a \emph{rim run}) or is
shared with exactly one sibling $X_{c'}$ (an \emph{interior run}). In the second case it is also a run of $X_{c'}$, its
\emph{partner}, traversed in the opposite direction, because marks are points and the same marks bound it on both
sides. At an interior vertex $u$ of an interior run, $X_c$ and $X_{c'}$ share both edges at $u$, so the full angle at $u$
is filled by these two children and no third child touches $u$. Since crossover corners are marks, the Zigzag Lemma
applies to every interior run.

\begin{definition}[Skeleton]\label{def:skeleton}
The \emph{skeleton} $\Sk$ of the composition $P=\bigcup X_c$ consists of the following data: the children and their
kinds; for each child, the cyclic sequence of its marks and runs, and which of its marks are $A_c$ and $B_c$; for each
interior run, its partner run and the parity of its length; the cyclic order of the rim runs round $\rim P$.
\end{definition}

Two skeletons of compositions with the same children (by number and kind) are \emph{equal} if there is a bijection of
their marks that preserves, for each child, the cyclic order of its marks and the labels $A_c,B_c$, and preserves
partner runs, the parities of interior runs and the cyclic order of the rim runs. The maps $\tau$ and $\sigma$ and the
parent ports below are defined from these data alone, so such a bijection carries them, and hence the conclusions of
\cref{prop:reduce}, from one composition to the other.

The \emph{reduced ports} are the ports $\Rp_c(u)$ and $\Lp_c(u)$ for every child $c$ and every mark $u$ on its rim. The
port $\Rp_c(u)$ lies on the run of $X_c$ that starts at $u$, and $\Lp_c(u)$ on the run that ends at $u$. Define two
involutions on reduced ports.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \emph{Inside a child}: $\tau(\Lp_c(u))=\Rp_c(u)$ if $u\notin\{A_c,B_c\}$; $\tau(\Rp_c(A_c))=\Rp_c(B_c)$ and
$\tau(\Lp_c(A_c))=\Lp_c(B_c)$.
\item \emph{Across an interior run} from $u$ to $u'$ of $X_c$, of length $n$, with partner in $X_{c'}$: if $n$ is
even, $\sigma(\Rp_c(u))=\Lp_c(u')$ and $\sigma(\Rp_{c'}(u'))=\Lp_{c'}(u)$; if $n$ is odd, $\sigma(\Rp_c(u))=\Rp_{c'}(u')$
and $\sigma(\Lp_c(u'))=\Lp_{c'}(u)$.
\end{itemize}
Both $\tau$ and $\sigma$ are determined by $\Sk$. The \emph{parent ports} are the reduced ports on rim runs. They are
exactly the rim ports $\Rp(u),\Lp(u)$ of $P$ at the rim vertices $u$ of $P$ that are marks (the \emph{parent marks}),
since $X_c$ and $P$ induce the same orientation on a rim run.

\begin{proposition}[Reduced composition]\label{prop:reduce}
Let $P=\bigcup X_c$, with every child having the signature. Then:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\alph*)}]
\item every rim vertex of $P$ that is not a mark is a hook of $P$;
\item for every reduced port $p$, the ribbon of $P$ followed from $p$ (first through its child) reaches the rim of
$P$ at the first parent port among $\tau(p),\ \tau\sigma\tau(p),\ \tau\sigma\tau\sigma\tau(p),\dots$, if there
is one (the ports $\sigma\tau(p),\dots$ in between lie on interior runs); for a parent port $p$ this is $\pi_P(p)$;
\item $\kappa(P)$ is the number of cycles of the alternation of $\sigma$ and $\tau$ that contain no parent port.
\end{enumerate}
In particular, $\pi_P$ at the parent marks and $\kappa(P)$ are determined by the skeleton.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
(a) Let $u$ be a rim vertex of $P$ that is not a mark. The rim edge of $P$ after $u$ lies in some $X_c$; if the
other rim edge of $X_c$ at $u$ were shared with a sibling, $u$ would be a junction. So both rim edges of $P$ at $u$
belong to $X_c$, and $u$ lies inside a rim run of $X_c$ and is not a crossover corner of $X_c$. Hence
$\pi_{X_c}(\Lp_c(u))=\Rp_c(u)$, and both ports are rim ports of $P$. (b) and (c) follow from \cref{lem:gluing}:
the ribbon starting at $p$ runs through $X_c$ to a reduced port $\tau(p)$, since by the signature of $X_c$ the arc of
$X_c$ starting at a port at a mark ends at a port at a mark. If $\tau(p)$ lies on an interior run, the ribbon then follows
that run, by the Zigzag Lemma, to $\sigma\tau(p)$. A closed strand of $P$ not inside a child must cross some shared
edge, and by the Zigzag Lemma it then passes through a reduced port; following it from there gives a cycle of the
alternation. Conversely each such cycle, read as arcs of children joined by zigzag connections, is a closed strand
of $P$, and distinct cycles give distinct strands. The children have no closed strands.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Where a hook goes]\label{cor:placement}
In the situation of \cref{prop:reduce}, let $u$ be a rim vertex of $X_c$ that is not a mark. If $u$ lies inside a rim
run, the hook of $X_c$ at $u$ is a hook of $P$. If $u$ lies inside an interior run, traversed by $X_c$ from $u_0$ to $u_n$, at distance $k$
from $u_0$, then the hook of $X_c$ at $u$ lies on the same arc or closed strand of $P$ as the reduced port $\Rp_c(u_0)$
if $k$ is even; if $k$ is odd, as $\Lp_c(u_n)$ when $n$ is odd and as $\Rp_{c'}(u_n)$ when $n$ is even.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
This is the last part of \cref{lem:zigzag}, together with \cref{prop:reduce}(a).
\end{proof}

edge of the hexagon in direction W, SW, SE, E, NE, NW for $j=0,\dots,5$.}
\label{tab:letters}

\begin{proposition}[Lengths by letter]\label{prop:letters}
For every $m\ge1$ the length $\ell_m(K,j)$ depends only on the letter of side $j$ of $K$. Writing $x_m$ for the common
length of the sides with letter $x$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rec}
\begin{aligned}
\alpha_{m+1}&=\beta_m+\epsilon_m+\eta_m, &\qquad \beta_{m+1}&=\alpha_m+\epsilon_m,\\
\gamma_{m+1}&=\beta_m+2\epsilon_m+\eta_m+\theta_m, & \delta_{m+1}&=\alpha_m+\beta_m+\epsilon_m+2\theta_m,\\
\epsilon_{m+1}&=3\epsilon_m+\eta_m, & \zeta_{m+1}&=\alpha_m+\epsilon_m+\theta_m,\\
\eta_{m+1}&=4\epsilon_m+\eta_m, & \theta_{m+1}&=4\epsilon_m+\eta_m+\theta_m,
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
with $(\alpha_1,\beta_1,\gamma_1,\delta_1,\epsilon_1,\zeta_1,\eta_1,\theta_1)=(7,5,10,10,8,6,10,11)$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For $m=1$ this is a direct count on the level-$1$ supertiles (\cref{fig:level1}; \cref{app:computations}). By
\cref{prop:pattern}(c), $\ell_{m+1}(T,J)$ is the sum of the lengths of the children's sides listed in
\cref{tab:psides}. Inspection of \cref{tab:children,tab:psides,tab:letters} shows that the multiset of letters of these children's
sides depends only on the letter of side $J$ of $T$, and is the one in~\eqref{eq:rec}. For example, side $0$ of
$\Delta$ (letter $\gamma$) is made of $7.5,6.4,6.3,6.2,5.4$, with letters $\beta,\eta,\epsilon,\epsilon,\theta$. The
claim follows by induction on $m$.
\end{proof}

The recurrence~\eqref{eq:rec} has a surprisingly simple solution. Let $\Fib_n$ be the Fibonacci numbers,
$\Fib_1=\Fib_2=1$.

\begin{proposition}[Fibonacci side lengths]\label{prop:fib}
For every $m\ge1$,
\begin{gather*}
\alpha_m=\Fib_{3m+2}+2,\qquad \beta_m=\Fib_{3m+1}+2,\qquad \gamma_m=\delta_m=\eta_m=2\Fib_{3m+2},\\
\epsilon_m=\Fib_{3m+3},\qquad \zeta_m=2\Fib_{3m+1},\qquad \theta_m=\Fib_{3m+4}-2 .
\end{gather*}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For $m=1$, $(\Fib_4,\Fib_5,\Fib_6,\Fib_7)=(3,5,8,13)$ gives the initial values. For the induction step put
$a=\Fib_{3m+1}$ and $b=\Fib_{3m+2}$, so that $\Fib_{3m+3}=a+b$, $\Fib_{3m+4}=a+2b$, $\Fib_{3m+5}=2a+3b$,
$\Fib_{3m+6}=3a+5b$ and $\Fib_{3m+7}=5a+8b$. Substituting into~\eqref{eq:rec}:
\begin{gather*}
\alpha_{m+1}=(a+2)+(a+b)+2b=\Fib_{3m+5}+2,\qquad \beta_{m+1}=(b+2)+(a+b)=\Fib_{3m+4}+2,\\
\gamma_{m+1}=(a+2)+2(a+b)+2b+(a+2b-2)=4a+6b=2\Fib_{3m+5},\\
\delta_{m+1}=(b+2)+(a+2)+(a+b)+2(a+2b-2)=4a+6b,\\ \eta_{m+1}=4(a+b)+2b=4a+6b,\qquad
\epsilon_{m+1}=3(a+b)+2b=\Fib_{3m+6},\\ \zeta_{m+1}=(b+2)+(a+b)+(a+2b-2)=2\Fib_{3m+4},\\
\theta_{m+1}=4(a+b)+2b+(a+2b-2)=5a+8b-2=\Fib_{3m+7}-2.\qedhere
\end{gather*}
\end{proof}

Since $\Fib_n$ is even exactly when $3\mid n$:

\begin{corollary}[Parities are constant]\label{cor:parity}
For every $m\ge1$ the lengths $\alpha_m$, $\beta_m$, $\theta_m$ are odd and $\gamma_m$, $\delta_m$, $\epsilon_m$,
$\zeta_m$, $\eta_m$ are even.
\end{corollary}

For example, the rim of a level-$m$ $\Delta$-supertile has length
$E_m(\Delta)=\alpha_m+\beta_m+2\gamma_m+\epsilon_m+\zeta_m$, which is $46,182,758,3198,13534,\dots$.

\subsection{The crossover corner}
\begin{lemma}[Position of $A$]\label{lem:Apos}
Let $m\ge1$. For $K\neq\Sigma$ the crossover corner $A$ of a level-$m$ $K$-supertile is its vertex $0$. For
$K=\Sigma$ it lies on side $5$ (letter $\delta$), at distance
\[
d_m=2\Fib_{3m+1}
\]
from vertex $5$ and at distance $\delta_m-d_m=2\Fib_{3m}$ from vertex $0$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
For $K\neq\Sigma$ this is \cref{prop:pattern}(d) for $m\ge2$, and is read off \cref{fig:level1} for $m=1$. For
$m=1$, $d_1=6$ is read off the level-$1$ $\Sigma$-supertile. By \cref{prop:pattern}(d) and
\cref{tab:psides}, the crossover corner of a level-$(m+1)$ $\Sigma$-supertile follows the children's sides $0.2$,
$0.1$, $7.0$ on its side~$5$. Child $0$ is a $\Xi$ and child $7$ a $\Gamma$, so these sides have letters
$\theta,\epsilon,\alpha$ and
$d_{m+1}=\theta_m+\epsilon_m+\alpha_m=(a+2b-2)+(a+b)+(b+2)=2\Fib_{3m+4}$, in the notation of the previous proof.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Lower bounds]\label{lem:bounds}
For every $m\ge1$: every side has length at least $5$; $d_m\ge6$; and $\delta_m-d_m\ge4$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
By \cref{prop:fib} all letter lengths increase with $m$, and at $m=1$ the smallest is $\beta_1=5$. Also $d_m\ge d_1=6$ and
$\delta_m-d_m=2\Fib_{3m}\ge 2\Fib_3=4$.
\end{proof}

\section{The signature at every level}\label{sec:induction}

This section proves the Signature Theorem and \cref{thm:A}. \Cref{sec:comp} describes how a supertile is glued from its
children purely in terms of the side lengths $\ell_m$ and the offset $d_m$ of \cref{sec:lengths}. \Cref{sec:lengthen}
shows, with the Zigzag Lemma, that lengthening a side by two edges preserves the signature of such a composition, and
that the lengths at every level are reached from those at level~$1$ by such steps; so one finite computation at
level~$1$ suffices. \Cref{sec:induct} runs the induction on the level, and \cref{sec:noclosed} deduces \cref{thm:A}.

\subsection{Compositions as a function of side lengths}\label{sec:comp}
Fix a parent kind $T$, with children $c=0,\dots,n_T-1$ of kinds $K_c$ (\cref{tab:children}); exactly one of them, $c_\Sigma$,
is a $\Sigma$. Let $L$ assign a positive integer $L(c,j)$ to each child $c$ and side $j\in\{0,\dots,5\}$, so that
sides glued by \cref{tab:sides} have the same length; let $d$ be an integer with $2\le d\le L(c_\Sigma,5)-2$; and let
$\omega\in\{\pm1\}$. The \emph{composition} $C_T(L,d,\omega)$ is the following abstract patch of boxes. Child $c$ has
a rim of $E_c=\sum_jL(c,j)$ vertices, numbered $0,\dots,E_c-1$ in its own order; side $j$ runs from vertex
$V_c(j)=\sum_{i<j}L(c,i)$ to vertex $V_c(j+1)$. Its crossover corner $A_c$ is vertex $0$, or for $c=c_\Sigma$ the
vertex at distance $d$ from vertex $5$ along side $5$, and $B_c=A_c+\omega$. Each child is a box with the signature at
$(A_c,B_c)$ (\cref{def:signature}). Sides are glued in pairs as in \cref{tab:sides}, reversing direction: the $o$-th
edge of side $j$ of $c$ is the $o$-th edge from the end of side $j'$ of $c'$. The sides marked $\rim$ form the rim of
the composition, in the order of \cref{tab:psides}; a rim vertex of the composition is the point between two
consecutive rim edges, and its ports $\Rp$ and $\Lp$ are the ports, on these two edges, of the children they belong
to. Ribbons are followed as in \cref{lem:gluing}: alternately through
a child, by its signature, and straight across a glued edge, until the rim is reached. This defines the box of the
composition: the pairing of its rim ports and its closed strands. \Cref{lem:zigzag,prop:reduce,cor:placement} and their
proofs apply unchanged to compositions (with marks, runs and skeletons defined as in \cref{def:marks,def:skeleton}),
since they use only the signatures of the children and the straight-through rule. We say that $C_T(L,d,\omega)$ \emph{has the
signature} if it has no closed strand, and every rim vertex is a hook except the crossover corners $A_\Gamma,B_\Gamma$
of its $\Gamma$ child, which are adjacent rim vertices ($B_\Gamma$ following $A_\Gamma$ in the children's order if
$\omega=+1$, and preceding it if $\omega=-1$) and carry the crossover.

Write $\ell_m$ also for the function $(c,j)\mapsto\ell_m(K_c,j)$. By \cref{prop:pattern,prop:letters,lem:Apos}, if
the children of a level-$(m+1)$ $T$-supertile have the signature at $(A_c,A_c+\omega_m)$ in their own orientation,
then the supertile is glued from its children as $C_T(\ell_m,d_m,\omega_m)$ is (with all children in their own
orientation).
Indeed: (i) by \cref{prop:pattern}(a),(b) and \cref{prop:letters} the children have the kinds and side lengths of the
composition, and side $j$ of $X_c$ is side $j'$ of $X_{c'}$ traversed oppositely, so points are matched as above;
(ii) the rims of two children meet only in the closed sides they share: at a point inside a side both rim edges are
shared with the partner, so the two children fill a neighbourhood, and a vertex lies on exactly three level-$m$
supertiles, any two of which share a side ending there (\cref{thm:smkgs}(b)); (iii) so each side of a child is wholly
shared with one sibling or wholly on the rim, as in \cref{tab:sides}; (iv) $A_c$ is placed by \cref{lem:Apos}, and
$B_c=A_c+\omega_m$ by hypothesis; (v) the rim of the parent is given by \cref{tab:psides} (\cref{prop:pattern}(c)).
Hence the children's rims, their corners and the gluing of their sides are those of $C_T(\ell_m,d_m,\omega_m)$. By
\cref{lem:gluing}, the strands of the supertile, followed from child to child, are then those of the composition; in
particular the box of the supertile is the box of $C_T(\ell_m,d_m,\omega_m)$.

\subsection{Lengthening}\label{sec:lengthen}
Let $(L,d)$ be as above. A \emph{move} lengthens one side of one child by two edges, together with the side glued to it
if there is one. An edge $e$ of that side is replaced by a path of three edges through two new vertices; $e$ must not
be the first or the last edge of its side (in each child that contains it), nor one of the two edges at $A_\Sigma$
(the crossover corner of the $\Sigma$ child). The result is $(L',d')$, where $L'$ is $L$ with the side (and its
partner) two edges longer, and $d'=d+2$ if $e$ lies on side $5$ of the $\Sigma$ child before $A_\Sigma$, and $d'=d$
otherwise.

\begin{lemma}[Lengthening]\label{lem:lengthen}
If $C_T(L,d,\omega)$ has the signature, then so does $C_T(L',d',\omega)$, for every move $(L,d)\to(L',d')$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Write $C=C_T(L,d,\omega)$ and $C'=C_T(L',d',\omega)$, and let $u_0u_1$ be the edge $e$. In $C'$ it is replaced by the
path $u_0v_1v_2u_1$; positions after $e$ on the rim of each child containing it move on by two, and every vertex of $C$
has an image vertex in $C'$. The corners of $C'$ are the images of those of $C$: for $A_\Sigma$ this is the choice of
$d'$, and $B_c=A_c\pm1$ stays next to $A_c$, because the edge between them (the first edge of side $0$, the last of
side $5$, or an edge at $A_\Sigma$) is never cut. In particular $v_1$ and $v_2$ are not crossover corners. Let $\varphi$
map each port of $C$ to the port of $C'$ of the same hand at the image vertex. Then $\varphi$ commutes with the
pairings inside the children, since the corners correspond, and with the straight-through rule across every glued edge
other than $e$. If $e$ is shared by children $c$ and $c'$, crossing it joins $\Rp_c(u_0)$ to $\Rp_{c'}(u_1)$ and
$\Lp_c(u_1)$ to $\Lp_{c'}(u_0)$ (the Zigzag Lemma, \cref{lem:zigzag}, with $n=1$); in $C'$, by \cref{lem:zigzag} with $n=3$, whose
interior vertices $v_1,v_2$ are hooks of both children, the ribbon from $\varphi(\Rp_c(u_0))$ runs along the new path
to $\varphi(\Rp_{c'}(u_1))$, and the ribbon from $\varphi(\Lp_c(u_1))$ to $\varphi(\Lp_{c'}(u_0))$. If $e$ lies on the
rim, $v_1$ and $v_2$ are hooks of the child, and so new rim vertices of $C'$ that are hooks of $C'$.

Hence, for every port $x$ of $C$, the ribbon of $C'$ from $\varphi(x)$ passes through the images of the ports met from
$x$ in $C$, and reaches the rim at the image of the rim port reached in $C$. So the pairing of the rim ports of $C'$ at
the images of the rim vertices of $C$ is the image of that of $C$: a hook at every one of them except $A_\Gamma$ and
$B_\Gamma$, which are adjacent as before and carry the crossover; and the new rim vertices, if any, are hooks. Finally,
$C'$ has no closed strand. Every port of $C'$ at an old vertex is some $\varphi(x)$, whose ribbon reaches the rim. A
strand through a port at $v_1$ or $v_2$ either is a hook of the rim (if $e$ lies on the rim), or follows one of the two
connections of \cref{lem:zigzag} along $u_0v_1v_2u_1$, which use every port on these edges and end at ports at $u_0$
and $u_1$; so it too reaches the rim.
\end{proof}

Call $(L,d)$ \emph{admissible} if sides glued by \cref{tab:sides} have the same
length; $L(c,j)\ge\ell_1(c,j)$ and $L(c,j)\equiv\ell_1(c,j)\pmod2$ for all $c$ and $j$; $d\ge d_1$ and $d\equiv
d_1\pmod2$; and $L(c_\Sigma,5)-d\ge\ell_1(c_\Sigma,5)-d_1=4$. Then $d\ge6$ and $L(c_\Sigma,5)-d\ge4$, so the composition
$C_T(L,d,\omega)$ is defined.

\begin{lemma}[Reachability]\label{lem:reach}
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\alph*)}]
\item For every $m\ge1$, the pair $(\ell_m,d_m)$ is admissible.
\item Every admissible $(L,d)\neq(\ell_1,d_1)$ arises by a move from an admissible pair $(L_0,d_0)$ with
$\sum_{c,j}L_0(c,j)<\sum_{c,j}L(c,j)$. The move can be chosen so that its edge $e$ lies at least two edges from both
ends of its side, in each child that contains it, unless that side is side $5$ of the $\Sigma$ child or is glued to
it.
\end{enumerate}
Hence every admissible pair is reached from $(\ell_1,d_1)$ by finitely many such moves through admissible pairs.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
(a) Glued sides have the same letter (\cref{sec:lengths}), so the same length. By \cref{prop:fib} the letter lengths do
not decrease with $m$, and by \cref{cor:parity} their parities do not change. By \cref{lem:Apos}, $d_m=2\Fib_{3m+1}\ge
6=d_1$ is even, and $\ell_m(c_\Sigma,5)-d_m=\delta_m-d_m=2\Fib_{3m}\ge4$.

(b) If $L=\ell_1$, then $L(c_\Sigma,5)=\delta_1=10$ and $d_1\le d\le 10-4$, so $d=d_1$. Hence $L\neq\ell_1$, and by the
parity condition some side $j$ of some child $c$ has $L(c,j)\ge\ell_1(c,j)+2$. We undo a move on that side. If the side
is neither side $5$ of $\Sigma$ nor glued to it, take $d_0=d$ and the move whose edge is the third edge of the side (the
third from the end in the partner); before the move the side has length at least $\ell_1(c,j)\ge5$
(\cref{lem:bounds}), so this edge lies at least two edges from both ends, in either child. Otherwise we undo a move on side $5$ of the
$\Sigma$ child (and the side glued to it, which belongs to another child), measuring positions along side $5$ from its
vertex $5$. If $d\ge d_1+2$, take $d_0=d-2$ and the move whose edge is the second edge of the side, before
$A_\Sigma$; it does not touch $A_\Sigma$, since $d_0\ge6$. If $d=d_1$, take $d_0=d$ and the second edge after
$A_\Sigma$; it is not the last edge of the side, since $L(c_\Sigma,5)-d\ge\ell_1(c_\Sigma,5)+2-d_1=6$. In each case $(L_0,d_0)$ is admissible, and $\sum L_0=\sum L-2$ or $\sum L-4$.
\end{proof}

\begin{computation}[The compositions at the level-$1$ lengths]\label{comp:compose}
For each of the nine kinds $T$ and both signs $\omega$, the composition $C_T(\ell_1,d_1,\omega)$ has the signature.
\end{computation}

This was computed twice: by a program that builds the $18$ compositions from \cref{tab:children,tab:sides,tab:psides}
and the lengths alone, follows the ribbon from every one of their ports and checks each condition of the signature
(\cref{app:computations}), and in the kernel of the Lean proof assistant (\cref{sec:intro}, \emph{Formal
verification}). Each composition has $8$ children ($7$ for $\Gamma$), $732$ ports ($640$) and a rim of $182$ edges
($160$). As a negative control, with each child's corner $B_c$ placed two vertices from $A_c$ instead of one, all
$18$ checks fail.

\begin{proposition}[Compositions at every level]\label{prop:compose}
For every $m\ge1$, every kind $T$ and both signs $\omega$, the composition $C_T(\ell_m,d_m,\omega)$ has the signature.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By \cref{comp:compose} and \cref{lem:lengthen}, every composition reached from $(\ell_1,d_1)$ by moves has the
signature. By \cref{lem:reach} this includes $C_T(\ell_m,d_m,\omega)$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The induction}\label{sec:induct}
\begin{computation}[Base case]\label{comp:base}
Every level-$1$ supertile has the signature at $(A,B)$, where $A$ is as in \cref{lem:Apos} and $B=A+\omega_1$ in own
order. Moreover $\ell_1$ and $d_1$ are as stated in \cref{prop:letters,lem:Apos}.
\end{computation}

This was computed from the tiles, using \cref{lem:gluing}, in exact arithmetic; the program checks the statement as
worded: the two non-hooks are the crossover corners of the $\Gamma$ child (the Mystic), they are adjacent,
$B=A+\omega_1$, they carry the crossover, and there is no closed strand. A second, separate program, in floating
point, confirms it (\cref{app:computations}). The level-$1$ supertiles, as glued patches of tiles, are also the input
of the Lean formalisation, whose kernel checks this computation again. The rims have $E_1=44$ edges for $\Gamma$ and
$46$ for the other kinds.

\begin{theorem}[Signature Theorem]\label{thm:signature}
In every homochiral tiling by $\Tile$, every level-$m$ supertile of every kind has the signature at $(A,B)$, where $A$
is its crossover corner (\cref{sec:own}; placed as in \cref{lem:Apos} for $m\ge1$) and $B=A+\omega_m$ in its own
order. In particular no supertile contains a closed strand.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
For $m=0$ this is \cref{lem:tilesig}, and for $m=1$ it is \cref{comp:base}. Let $m\ge1$, assume the theorem for level
$m$, and let $Y$ be a level-$(m+1)$ supertile of kind $T$. Its children have the signature at $(A_c,A_c+\omega_m)$, so
by \cref{sec:comp} its box is that of $C_T(\ell_m,d_m,\omega_m)$, which has the signature by \cref{prop:compose}.
Hence $Y$ has no closed strand, and hooks at all its rim vertices except the crossover corners $A_\Gamma,B_\Gamma$ of
its $\Gamma$ child, which are adjacent and carry the crossover. By definition $A_\Gamma$ is the crossover corner of
$Y$, placed as in \cref{lem:Apos}. Finally $B_\Gamma=A_\Gamma+\omega_m$ in the child's own order, which is opposite to
that of $Y$; so $B=A-\omega_m=A+\omega_{m+1}$ in the own order of $Y$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:ccw}
Since the own orientation alternates with $m$ and so does $\omega_m$, in absolute terms $B$ is always the
counter-clockwise neighbour of $A$ on the rim, as it is for the tile ($B=v_1$).
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Other checks of the signature]\label{rem:skeleton}
Of the computations in this section the proof uses only \cref{comp:compose,comp:base}. The following computations
and arguments agree with them, and are not used. (1)~The compositions $C_T(\ell_m,d_m,\pm1)$ were built and followed port by port for $m\le4$; each of the
$1{,}792$ single moves from $(\ell_1,d_1)$ was checked to keep the signature, with both signs $\omega$; and the moves
that the proof of \cref{lem:reach} chooses were followed from $(\ell_2,d_2)$ and $(\ell_3,d_3)$ back to
$(\ell_1,d_1)$ for every kind, each one checked to be a move and, from $(\ell_2,d_2)$, every composition on the way
checked to have the signature. (2)~The signature of the actual
supertiles was computed from the tiles at levels $1$ to $3$ (and $4$) in exact arithmetic, and at levels $1$ to $9$ in
floating point, by the two programs of \cref{app:computations}. (3)~A second route to \cref{prop:compose} uses the
skeleton (\cref{def:skeleton}). For letter lengths $\lambda$ (and $C_T(\lambda,d,\omega)$ the composition
with $L(c,j)=\lambda$ of the letter of side $j$ of $K_c$) with $\lambda(x)\ge3$ and $3\le d\le\lambda(\delta)-3$,
the marks on each side of a child lie at the symbolic positions $0<1<d-1<d<d+1<\ell-1<\ell$ or
$0<1<\ell-d-1<\ell-d<\ell-d+1<\ell-1<\ell$, whose order does not depend on the lengths, and the length of a run is a
sum of side lengths plus a difference of symbolic positions. So the skeleton of $C_T(\lambda,d,\omega)$ depends only on
$\lambda\bmod2$, $d\bmod2$ and $\omega$, and by \cref{prop:reduce} so do its pairing at the parent marks and its
closed strands (every other parent rim vertex is a hook). The $18$
skeletons at $(\ell_1,d_1,\pm1)$ have $24$ marks ($21$ for $\Gamma$) and $52$ runs ($45$), of which $10$ ($9$) lie on
the rim; their reduced walks give the signature, and they coincide with the skeletons of the actual supertiles at
every level from $2$ to $9$ (a comparison that does not distinguish the parent's $A$ from its $B$; the children's are
distinguished).
\end{remark}

\subsection{No closed strands}\label{sec:noclosed}
\begin{proof}[Proof of \cref{thm:A}]
Let $\T$ be a homochiral tiling whose hierarchy covers the plane: for some tile $t$, the supertiles
$X_0(t)\subset X_1(t)\subset\cdots$ containing $t$ have union $\RR^2$. Every tile $t'$ lies in some $X_m(t)$: a
point $x$ of the interior of $t'$ lies in $X_m(t)$ for some $m$, hence in some tile $s$ of $X_m(t)$. Every neighbourhood
of $x$ meets the interior of $s$, so the interiors of $s$ and $t'$ meet, and $s=t'$. A closed strand consists of finitely many pieces in finitely many tiles, so it lies in a single $X_m(t)$, and
all its joins are across interior edges of $X_m(t)$. It is then a closed strand of $X_m(t)$, contradicting
\cref{thm:signature}. By \cref{lem:degree2}, every strand is a two-way infinite path.
\end{proof}

\section{The nest tiling}\label{sec:nest}

This section builds the nest tiling $\N$, grown from one tile by taking each block as child $1$ of the next, and shows
that it is a tiling of the plane with no closed strand (\cref{prop:nesttiling}). Child $1$ of every supertile is a
$\Delta$ (\cref{tab:children}).

\begin{construction}[The nest]\label{con:nest}
Let $\T$ be a homochiral tiling~\cite[Thm.~2.2]{SMKGS-spectre}. For each $h\ge0$, let $Y^{(h)}$ be a level-$h$ $\Delta$-supertile
of $\T$; one exists, for instance child $1$ of any level-$(h+1)$ supertile. Let
$Y^{(h)}=Y^{(h)}_h\supset Y^{(h)}_{h-1}\supset\cdots\supset Y^{(h)}_0$ be its chain of descendants, each child $1$ of
the next. Let $g_h$ be the proper motion taking the tile $Y^{(h)}_0$ onto $\Tile$ (with $v_0$ at the origin), and put
$N^{(h)}_b=g_h(Y^{(h)}_b)$ for $b\le h$.
\end{construction}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:nestwell}
$N^{(h)}_b$ does not depend on $h\ge b$ (or on the choices made). Writing $N_b$ for it, $N_0=\{\Tile\}$, and $N_b$ is
child $1$ of $N_{b+1}$ for every $b$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let $h,h'\ge b$, with $Y^{(h)}$, $Y^{(h')}$, $g_h$, $g_{h'}$ as in \cref{con:nest}. By \cref{lem:rigid} there is a proper motion $f$ with $f(Y^{(h)}_b)=Y^{(h')}_b$ that maps descendants to
descendants with their numbers; hence $f(Y^{(h)}_{b'})=Y^{(h')}_{b'}$ for all $b'\le b$, in particular for the tiles
$Y_0$. So $g_{h'}f$ and $g_h$ both take the tile $Y^{(h)}_0$ onto $\Tile$, and since a tile has no symmetry they
are equal. Thus $N^{(h')}_b=g_{h'}f(Y^{(h)}_b)=N^{(h)}_b$.
\end{proof}

The \emph{nest tiling} is $\N=\bigcup_{b\ge0}N_b$, a set of tiles. \Cref{fig:nest} shows three consecutive blocks.

\begin{lemma}[Grandparent Lemma]\label{lem:gap}
Let $b\ge1$. \textup{(a)} The block $N_b$ does not meet the rim of $N_{b+2}$. \textup{(b)} Every edge of a tile of
$N_b$ is shared by two tiles of $N_{b+2}$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
(a) $N_b$ is child $1$ of $N_{b+1}$ (\cref{prop:nestwell}). By \cref{tab:sides}, the sides $0,1,3,4,5$ of child $1$ face the siblings $7,0,2,3,4$, and side $2$ lies on the parent's
rim. A point of $N_b$ that is not on its closed side $2$ is therefore an interior point of $N_{b+1}$. This is clear
for interior points of $N_b$ and for interior points of the other sides, where $N_b$ and one sibling together fill a
neighbourhood. At the vertices $0,1,4,5$ of $N_b$, exactly three level-$b$ supertiles meet; two of them are the
distinct siblings across the adjacent sides, and together they fill a neighbourhood. Hence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:side2}
N_b\cap\rim N_{b+1}=\text{closed side } 2 \text{ of } N_b .
\end{equation}
By \cref{tab:psides} (the $\Delta$ row: positions $14$ to $18$), side $4$ of the parent is the concatenation of
$2.2,2.1,1.2,0.4,0.3$. Since $1.2$ is neither first nor last, the closed side $2$ of $N_b$ lies in the relative
interior of side $4$ of $N_{b+1}$. Now let $p\in N_b\cap\rim N_{b+2}$. Then $p\in\rim N_{b+1}$, for otherwise $p$ would be
interior to $N_{b+1}\subset N_{b+2}$. So $p$ lies on side $4$ of $N_{b+1}$ by the above, and, by~\eqref{eq:side2} for
$N_{b+1}$, on the closed side $2$ of $N_{b+1}$. But the closed sides $2$ and $4$ of $N_{b+1}$ are disjoint: they are
arcs of a simple closed curve separated by side $3$ on one side and by sides $5,0,1$ on the other.

(b) follows from (a). It also follows from the tables alone, and this is how the formalisation proves it: a rim
edge of $N_b$ lies on a side $j\ne2$ of $N_b$, which faces a sibling inside $N_{b+1}$, or on side $2$, which lies on
side $4$ of $N_{b+1}$ (position $16$ of \cref{tab:psides}); and side $4$ of child $1$ faces sibling $3$ inside $N_{b+2}$
(\cref{tab:sides}).
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:nesttiling}
$\N$ is a homochiral tiling of the plane, and every tile of $\N$ lies in some $N_b$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Any two tiles of $\N$ lie in a common block, so their interiors are disjoint. We show that the tiles cover the plane.
Fix $h$ and work in the homochiral tiling $\T_h=g_h(\T)$ of \cref{con:nest}, which contains $N_0,\dots,N_h$
(\cref{prop:nestwell}). Call two tiles of $\T_h$
\emph{neighbours} if they share a point, and let $\operatorname{dist}$ be the resulting graph distance. We claim that every
tile at distance at most $k$ from $N_1$ lies in $N_{1+2k}$, provided $1+2k\le h$. For $k=0$ there is nothing to prove.
If $t$ has distance $k+1$, it shares a point $p$ with a tile of $N_{1+2k}$. By \cref{lem:gap}, $p$ is an interior point of
$N_{3+2k}$, so the interior of $t$ meets the interior of a tile of $N_{3+2k}$; hence $t$ is that tile.
Now let $x\in\RR^2$ and let $D$ be the disc of radius $r=|x|+1$ about the origin. The tiles of $\T_h$ meeting $D$ have
diameter $3+\sqrt3<5$ and area $3+3\sqrt3>8$, so there are at most $k_0=\lfloor\pi(r+5)^2/8\rfloor$ of them. They cover
the connected set $D$, so their neighbour graph is connected. One of them is $N_0\subset N_1$, since the origin is a
vertex of $N_0$. So every tile of $\T_h$ meeting $D$, in particular one containing $x$, lies in $N_{1+2k_0}$ as soon as
$h\ge 1+2k_0$. Since $N_{1+2k_0}$ does not depend on $h$, $x$ is covered by $\N$.
\end{proof}

The blocks $N_b$ are supertiles in the sense of \cref{sec:spectre}: each is congruent, with all its structure, to a
level-$b$ $\Delta$-supertile of $\T$. So \cref{thm:signature} applies to them. In particular $\N$ has no closed strand: a closed strand passes through
finitely many tiles, which lie in a common block $N_b$ by \cref{prop:nesttiling}, and would be a closed strand of
$N_b$.

\section{Merging and the single strand}\label{sec:strand}

This section shows that the arcs of every block of the nest merge within three or four levels (\cref{sec:merging}),
and proves \cref{thm:B} (\cref{sec:onestrand}). The merging uses the device of \cref{sec:induction}: a map computed
once at the level-$1$ lengths and carried to every level by the moves of \cref{lem:reach}.

\subsection{Merging}\label{sec:merging}
For $b\ge1$ and $k\ge0$, every arc of $N_b$ is contained in a unique arc of $N_{b+k}$, because $N_{b+k}$ has no closed
strand. Let $\mathcal P_k(b)$ be the partition of the $E_b(\Delta)$ arcs of $N_b$ in which two arcs are in the same block
when they lie on the same arc of $N_{b+k}$. The blocks only merge as $k$ increases.

\begin{theorem}[Merging]\label{thm:merge}
For every $b\ge1$, $\mathcal P_4(b)$ has a single block; if $b$ is even, so does $\mathcal P_3(b)$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{computation}[The junction map at the level-$1$ lengths]\label{comp:junction}
In $C_\Delta(\ell_1,d_1,\omega)$, for both signs $\omega$, with child $1$ in the place of $N_m$ and positions and
junctions defined in the same way: every rim vertex of child $1$ strictly inside its side $2$ is a hook of the
composition; and the ribbon from every other port of child $1$ reaches the rim of the composition at junction $q$,
where $q$ is given by \cref{tab:junction} with $\omega_m=\omega$.
\end{computation}

This was computed twice, like \cref{comp:compose}: by the program of \cref{app:computations}, and in the kernel of the
Lean proof assistant (its second part; the formalisation proves the first for every level, from the signature of
child~$1$). As a negative control, exchanging the junctions $12$ and $16$ in \cref{tab:junction} makes the
check fail for both signs.

\begin{lemma}[The junction map]\label{lem:junctionmap}
Let $m\ge1$. In $N_{m+1}$, every hook of $N_m$ at a rim vertex strictly inside side $2$ of $N_m$ is a hook of
$N_{m+1}$, and every other arc of $N_m$ lies on the hook $H_q$ of $N_{m+1}$, where $q$ is given by
\cref{tab:junction}.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Say that $(L,d)$ has the \emph{junction property} if the statement of \cref{comp:junction} holds for
$C_\Delta(L,d,\omega)$ for both $\omega$. The children of $N_{m+1}$ have the signature (\cref{thm:signature}), so by
\cref{sec:comp} $N_{m+1}$ is glued from them as $C_\Delta(\ell_m,d_m,\omega_m)$ is, with $N_m$ as child $1$; and a ribbon of the composition from a port $p$ of child $1$
runs through child $1$ along the arc of $N_m$ ending at $p$, and on along the arc of $N_{m+1}$ that contains it. A hook
arc has both its ends at the same rim vertex. So the lemma says that $(\ell_m,d_m)$ has the junction property. By
\cref{comp:junction}, $(\ell_1,d_1)$ has it. By \cref{lem:reach}, $(\ell_m,d_m)$ is reached from $(\ell_1,d_1)$ by
moves as in \cref{lem:reach}(b) through admissible pairs, so it suffices to show that such a move keeps the junction
property. Child $1$ is a $\Delta$, and the side glued to side $5$ of the $\Sigma$ child (child $4$) belongs to child $7$
(\cref{tab:sides}); so a move whose edge lies on the rim of child $1$ keeps it at least two edges from both ends of its
side.

Let $C$, $C'$ and $\varphi$ be as in the proof of \cref{lem:lengthen}. Both have the signature (by the proof of
\cref{prop:compose}), and the junctions of $C'$
are the images of those of $C$, since junctions are vertices of children; so are the vertices $V_j$ of child $1$, and
$\varphi$ keeps the order of positions on its rim. For a port $x$ of child $1$ at an old vertex, the ribbon from
$\varphi(x)$ reaches the rim at the image of the rim port reached from $x$, and $\varphi(x)$ lies in the same row of
\cref{tab:junction} as $x$. It remains to consider the new vertices $v_1,v_2$ when the edge $e=u_0u_1$ lies on the rim
of child $1$. Then $v_1,v_2,u_0,u_1$ lie strictly inside the same side of child $1$, at least two edges from its ends,
so in the same row of \cref{tab:junction}, and none is a crossover corner. If $e$ lies on the rim of the composition
(that is, on side $2$ of child $1$), $v_1$ and $v_2$ are hooks of $C'$, as the table says. Otherwise, by the last part
of the Zigzag Lemma (\cref{lem:zigzag}, with $n=3$), the hooks of child $1$ at $v_1$ and $v_2$ lie on the strands of $C'$ through
$\varphi$ of the ports of child $1$ at $u_1$ and $u_0$. Those ports lie in the same row, so these strands are arcs of
$C'$ with an end at the junction that the row names; an arc with an end at a hook is that hook, and both its ends are
at the junction (junction $q$ is a hook of $C'$: $C'$ has the signature, and junction $q$ is at least one child side,
so at least five edges, along the rim from junction $0$, which is $A_\Gamma$, so it is neither $A_\Gamma$ nor
$B_\Gamma$). So the ribbons from the ports at $v_1$ and $v_2$ reach the rim there too.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Where the junctions lie]\label{lem:junctions}
Let $m\ge1$. The junctions $9$, $12$, $16$, $17$ and $21$ of $N_{m+1}$ lie on its sides $2$, $3$, $4$, $4$ and $5$
respectively, at least five edges from both ends of the side. Every rim vertex strictly inside side $2$ of $N_m$ lies on
side $4$ of $N_{m+1}$, at least five edges from both ends.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
By \cref{tab:psides}, side $2$ of a $\Delta$ is made of the children's sides at positions $7$ to $10$, side $3$ of
those at $11$ to $13$, side $4$ of those at $14$ to $18$, and side $5$ of those at $19$ to $21$. Junction $q$ lies
between the sides at positions $q-1$ and $q$, which lie in the same side of $N_{m+1}$ for $q=9,12,16,17,21$; and side $2$
of $N_m$ is the side at position $16$. Every side of a supertile of level $\ge1$ has at least five edges
(\cref{lem:bounds}).
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of \cref{thm:merge}]
A rim vertex of $N_m$ at least two edges from both ends of its side $J$ is not a crossover corner, and by
\cref{lem:junctionmap} its hook lies on the hook of $N_{m+1}$ that \cref{tab:junction} names for side $J$: for even
$m$, junction $17$, $17$, $16$, $12$, $9$ for $J=0,1,3,4,5$; for odd $m$, junction $21$, $17$, $16$, $16$, $16$; and
for $J=2$, the vertex is itself a hook of $N_{m+1}$, on its side $4$. By \cref{lem:junctions}, each of these hooks of
$N_{m+1}$ lies at least five edges inside a side of $N_{m+1}$, so the same rule applies to it one level up.

Let $b\ge1$ be even, so that $\omega_b=+1$. By \cref{lem:junctionmap} every arc of $N_b$ lies on $H_{17}$, $H_{16}$,
$H_{12}$ or $H_9$ of $N_{b+1}$, or on a hook of $N_{b+1}$ strictly inside side $2$ of $N_b$; by \cref{lem:junctions}
these are hooks inside sides $4$, $4$, $3$, $2$ and $4$ of $N_{b+1}$. Since $b+1$ is odd, the hooks inside sides $3$ and
$4$ lie on $H_{16}$ of $N_{b+2}$, and those inside side $2$ are hooks of $N_{b+2}$ inside its side $4$. All are inside
side $4$ of $N_{b+2}$, and since $b+2$ is even they lie on $H_{12}$ of $N_{b+3}$. So $\mathcal P_3(b)$ has a single
block.

Let $b\ge1$ be odd. Every arc of $N_b$ lies on $H_{21}$, $H_{17}$ or $H_{16}$ of $N_{b+1}$, or on a hook of $N_{b+1}$
strictly inside side $2$ of $N_b$: inside sides $5$, $4$, $4$, $4$ of $N_{b+1}$. Since $b+1$ is even, those inside side $4$
lie on $H_{12}$ and those inside side $5$ on $H_9$ of $N_{b+2}$, inside its sides $3$ and $2$. Since $b+2$ is odd, the
first lie on $H_{16}$ of $N_{b+3}$, and the second are hooks of $N_{b+3}$ inside its side $4$. All are inside side $4$ of
$N_{b+3}$, and since $b+3$ is even they lie on $H_{12}$ of $N_{b+4}$. So $\mathcal P_4(b)$ has a single block. Once
$\mathcal P_k(b)$ has a single block, so does $\mathcal P_{k'}(b)$ for every $k'\ge k$.

The same steps give the last two rows of \cref{tab:merge}. For even $b$ the arcs of $N_b$ lie on two arcs of
$N_{b+2}$: $H_{16}$, and the hook at junction $9$ of $N_{b+1}$, which lies inside side $2$ of $N_{b+1}$ and so is not
junction $16$ of $N_{b+2}$ (an end of that side). For odd $b$ they lie on $H_{12}$ and $H_9$ of $N_{b+2}$, and on
$H_{16}$ of $N_{b+3}$ and the hook at junction $9$ of $N_{b+2}$, which for the same reason is not junction $16$ of
$N_{b+3}$. Both arcs are reached in each case (for instance from
vertices well inside sides $4$ and $5$ of $N_b$, or sides $3$ and $0$), and distinct hooks are distinct arcs.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Other checks of \cref{thm:merge}]\label{rem:labels}
Besides \cref{comp:compose,comp:base}, the proof of \cref{thm:merge} uses only \cref{comp:junction}.
The following agree with it, and are not used. (1)~\Cref{tab:merge} was computed on the actual blocks for $b\le6$, following every ribbon up to level $9$, and
for $b\le3$ by a second program in exact arithmetic up to level $7$. (2)~The junction map was checked directly on
$C_\Delta(\ell_m,d_m,\pm1)$ for $m\le4$, after each of the $190$ single moves from $(\ell_1,d_1)$ on the $\Delta$
composition whose edge, if it lies on child $1$, is at least two edges from the ends of its side (with both signs),
and on the actual blocks $N_1\subset N_2$, $N_2\subset N_3$, $N_3\subset N_4$, built from the tiles in exact
arithmetic. (3)~A second proof follows the arcs
of $N_b$ up the nest with \emph{label states}: finite records of which hooks and crossover arcs of $N_{b+k}$ carry arcs
of $N_b$, advanced one level at a time with the reduced walk (\cref{prop:reduce}) and \cref{cor:placement}, with every
position written as an integer affine function of $\ell_{b-1}$. Run symbolically over $8$ relative levels, once for each
parity of $b$, its branch decisions are $2{,}106$ linear conditions on the side lengths ($1{,}407$ distinct). For every
$b\ge2$, $1{,}980$ of them hold because every side has at least $5$ edges, and the other $126$ were certified twice:
from \cref{prop:fib} with Binet's formula, and by exact rational certificates on the space spanned by the side-length
vectors $\ell_1,\ell_2,\ell_3$, which contains every $\ell_{b-1}$. With $b=1$ computed directly, this gives
\cref{thm:merge} and the last two rows of \cref{tab:merge} again.
\end{remark}

\subsection{One strand}\label{sec:onestrand}
\begin{proof}[Proof of \cref{thm:B}]
By \cref{lem:degree2}, every component of the strand graph $\G(\N)$ is a cycle or a two-way infinite path, and there
are no cycles, as shown after \cref{prop:nesttiling}. It remains to show that $\G(\N)$ is connected.
Let $p,q$ be pieces of tiles $t,t'$ of $\N$. By \cref{prop:nesttiling} both tiles lie in some $N_b$ with $b\ge1$. Since
$N_b$ has no closed strand, $p$ and $q$ lie on arcs of $N_b$. By \cref{thm:merge} these arcs lie on one arc of $N_{b+4}$,
so $p$ and $q$ are on the same strand. The strand passes through every tile, since every tile has pieces.
\end{proof}

\section{Scope and open questions}\label{sec:scope}

\subsection*{Other tilings}
\Cref{thm:A} holds for every tiling whose hierarchy covers the plane. \Cref{thm:B} is proved for the nest tiling, whose
chain of supertiles is $\Delta\subset\Delta\subset\cdots$ with child number $1$ at every step. Every tiling whose
hierarchy covers the plane has some such chain $(K_m,c_m)_{m\ge1}$, where $X_m$ is a level-$m$ supertile of kind $K_m$
and child $c_m$ of $X_{m+1}$. The induction of \cref{sec:induction} does not see the chain, but the merging of
\cref{sec:strand} does: its finite part depends on the sequence $(K_m,c_m)$, and so may its outcome.

\begin{remark}[Three strands: a computation, not a theorem]\label{rem:three}
Let $K_m=\Theta$ for odd $m$ and $K_m=\Gamma$ for even $m$, with $c_m=2$ for odd $m$ and $c_m=7$ for even $m$
(\cref{tab:children}). A tiling with this chain is constructed as in \cref{con:nest}, and its hierarchy covers the plane:
the argument of \cref{lem:gap} and \cref{prop:nesttiling}, applied with \cref{tab:sides,tab:psides}, shows that no
$X_m$ meets the rim of $X_{m+2}$. In this tiling, following the ribbons through the actual supertiles in exact
arithmetic, the arcs of $X_1$ lie on exactly three arcs of $X_m$ for $3\le m\le8$, and the same holds for the arcs
of $X_2$ and of $X_3$ for $5\le m\le 8$. The label bookkeeping of \cref{rem:labels}, run along this chain, agrees and
still gives three at $m=20$. If this persists for every block and every level, the pieces of this tiling form exactly
three strands, each a two-way infinite path by \cref{thm:A}. We have not proved this, and the paper does not use it.
For the other alignment ($K_m=\Gamma$, $c_m=7$ for odd $m$ and $K_m=\Theta$, $c_m=2$ for even $m$) the arcs of
$X_1$ lie on a single arc of $X_4$. Along $90{,}000$ chains chosen at random step by step ($30{,}000$ for each
$b\le3$), the label bookkeeping put the arcs of $X_b$ on a single arc of $X_m$ for some $m\le20$ in all but five. The programs are in \code{experiments/}, with logs; the label bookkeeping along a chain is \code{hierarchy/chains\_label.js},
which agrees with exact strand following on $60$ chains up to level~$8$.
\end{remark}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,label=(\arabic*)]
\item Which homochiral tilings whose hierarchy covers the plane carry a single strand? Is the number of strands
always finite, and which numbers occur?
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Faults}
\Cref{thm:A} assumes that the hierarchy covers the plane. This subsection says what that leaves out, and why.

Take a tile $t$ and the supertiles $X_0(t)\subset X_1(t)\subset\cdots$ that contain it. If the hierarchy covers the plane,
they grow until they cover the whole plane. Then every closed strand, being made of finitely many pieces, lies inside one of them, and
the Signature Theorem (\cref{thm:signature}) rules it out: that is the whole proof of \cref{thm:A}
(\cref{sec:noclosed}). In a tiling with a \emph{fault} they grow for ever but fill only part of the plane, an
\emph{infinite supertile} (Tatham's term~\cite[\S5]{TathamTransducers}; Goodman-Strauss~\cite{GoodmanStrauss1999}
speaks of infinitely large supertiles). The rest of the plane is filled by other infinite supertiles, built in the same way from
other tiles. Two infinite supertiles do not overlap, and no supertile of any level reaches from one into another. The
boundaries between them run along tile edges, and they are the fault (\cref{fig:fault}).

A closed strand that stays inside one infinite supertile lies in a single supertile, and is ruled out as before. A
closed strand that crossed the fault, with pieces on both sides, would lie in no single supertile, and the argument
does not reach it. So \cref{thm:A} is stated only for tilings without a fault, and this paper makes no claim about
closed strands in tilings with a fault: it neither proves that there are none nor exhibits one. \Cref{thm:B} is not
affected, since the nest tiling has no fault (\cref{prop:nesttiling}).

Tilings with faults do exist, by the usual compactness argument; we sketch it. For each $m\ge1$, apply a proper motion to a homochiral tiling so that every tile is $\rho^k\Tile+t$
($k\in\ZZ$, $t\in\ZZ[\rho]$) and the origin is a vertex of a level-$m$ supertile; call the result $\T_m$. The origin
is then where three level-$m$ supertiles meet~\cite[Thm.~5.1]{SMKGS-spectre}, and it lies on the boundary of a
level-$n$ supertile for every $n\le m$. For fixed $r$ and $n$, since the tiles meet edge to edge, the origin is a tile vertex, and boundedly
many tiles meet the disc of radius $r$ about the origin, there are only finitely many possibilities for these tiles,
together with their grouping into level-$n'$ supertiles for $n'\le n$,
the kinds of these supertiles and their child numbers. By a diagonal argument, some subsequence of the $\T_m$ has all
these data eventually constant, for every $r$ and $n$. The limit is a homochiral tiling whose level-$n'$ groupings are
compositions as in \cref{prop:pattern}; by the uniqueness in \cref{thm:smkgs}(a) they are its supertiles. In it the origin lies on the boundary of a level-$n$
supertile for every $n$. The finitely many tiles at the origin cover a neighbourhood of it, so they never all lie in one
supertile, and they meet at least two different infinite supertiles. Tatham~\cite[\S7.3]{TathamTransducers} gives an
explicit example: a tiling with two infinite supertiles side by side, with addresses
$\Xi\xleftarrow{7}\Pi\xleftarrow{7}\Xi\xleftarrow{7}\cdots$ and $\Pi\xleftarrow{7}\Xi\xleftarrow{7}\Pi\xleftarrow{7}\cdots$
in his numbering of the children, almost all of which is invariant under a rotation by $60^\circ$.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,label=(\arabic*),resume]
\item Are there homochiral tilings by $\Tile$, necessarily with faults (\cref{thm:A}), in which the decoration has a
closed strand?
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Other drawings and designs}
The theorems concern only the pairing of ports (\cref{def:decoration}) and the straight-through rule. They therefore
apply to any drawing that realises these, including drawings on the curved Spectre, whose tilings correspond to the
homochiral tilings by $\Tile$~\cite[\S2]{SMKGS-spectre}. The method uses the decoration only through the signature of the tile (\cref{lem:tilesig}), the position of the
crossover, which fixes $A$ (\cref{lem:Apos}), the level-$1$ computations (\cref{comp:compose,comp:base}) and,
for \cref{thm:B}, the junction map (\cref{comp:junction}). For any other decoration in which
the tile has the signature at some edge, the same finite computations can be run; their outcome may differ.

\section{The computations}\label{app:computations}

All programs and their logs are in the ancillary files of this paper, folder \code{code/}. One command,
\code{./run\_all.sh}, re-runs everything the proof needs, with the quicker corroborating computations, in about twelve
minutes on a four-core machine; \code{./run\_all.sh -{}-all} adds the corroborating computations to level~$9$, and
takes about an hour in all (with the memory of the JavaScript programs capped at $6.5$\,GB). The file
\code{code/README.md} maps every statement of this paper to the script that checks it. The Lean formalisation is in the
folder \code{lean/}; its \code{build.sh} builds and checks it in about six minutes, and its \code{README.md} maps the
statements of this paper to the formal ones.

\subsection*{What the proof computes, and how}
Besides the comparisons with~\cite[Figs.~4.1, 5.1]{SMKGS-spectre} in the proof of \cref{prop:pattern}, the proof uses
two kinds of computation. The geometric computations on actual supertiles are the Mystic
(\cref{lem:tilesig}), the level-$1$ data (vertex positions, $\ell_1$, $d_1$, the position of $A$, and the signature of
the level-$1$ supertiles, \cref{comp:base}) and the patterns of the compositions $m\to m+1$ for $m\le3$. All of them
are done in exact arithmetic by \code{exact/exact.py}, in Python. It represents every vertex exactly as an element
$a+b\rho+c\rho^2+d\rho^3$ of $\ZZ[\rho]$, $\rho=e^{i\pi/6}$, with integers $a,b,c,d$, and every motion as
$z\mapsto\rho^kz+t$ (or $\rho^k\bar z+t$ in reflected frames). Floating point is used only to read off the sign of the
area of a polygon whose area exceeds $8$ in absolute value. The computations on compositions
(\cref{comp:compose,comp:junction}) use only \cref{tab:children,tab:sides,tab:psides} and the integers $\ell_1$, $d_1$.
The program \code{hierarchy/compositions.py} builds each composition, follows the ribbon from every port, and checks
the statement as worded. The Lean formalisation repeats both computations in its kernel, from the same tables, and
re-checks \cref{comp:base} on the level-$1$ supertiles exported by \code{exact.py} (in the table below, the Lean files
named are in \code{lean/SpectreKnot/}).

The JavaScript programs in \code{core/} and most of \code{hierarchy/} repeat the geometric computations independently
and extend them: the patterns for $m\le7$, the signature up to level $9$, the merging for $b\le6$ up to level $9$.
These extensions corroborate the proof but are not used in it. So do the skeleton walks, the symbolic run of the label
states and its certificates (\cref{rem:skeleton,rem:labels}). They work with integers and rationals only; the
integers involved are below $2\cdot10^7$ in absolute value, far below $2^{53}$, so the parts written in JavaScript are
exact as well. The inputs of the symbolic run (the pattern, the matrix $M$ and $\ell_1$) are read from the JavaScript
programs and checked equal to the exact ones. The programs share no code with \code{exact.py}, although
both implement the key-point construction of~\cite[App.~A]{SMKGS-spectre} and so the same table of placements. They
work in double-precision floating point and identify points by rounding coordinates to $10^{-2}$ or $10^{-3}$. We do
not bound their rounding error. A separate check (\code{crosscheck/overlap.js}), which builds the supertiles of levels $1$
to $7$ in the same way, finds distinct vertices at distance at least $0.89$, and points that it identifies within
$4\times10^{-12}$ of each other; and up to level~$9$ every rim found has exactly $E_m$ edges (\cref{prop:fib}) and every
rim vertex has degree $2$, which a wrongly identified point would break. All programs compose the ribbon pairings with
\cref{lem:gluing} and nothing else. (In the programs the sign $\omega_m$ is called \texttt{beta}.)
